What a radio does, and what "software-defined" changes

Start here even if it feels too basic.

A radio signal is a wave — a voltage that swings up and down very fast. How fast is its frequency, in hertz (Hz), cycles per second. A million per second is a megahertz (MHz); a billion is a gigahertz (GHz). This board works between 70 MHz and 6 GHz.

To send information you modulate the wave: change something about it in a pattern the other end can read — its height (amplitude), its timing (phase), or its frequency. That is all radio is.

The traditional way, and the software way

A conventional radio does this with dedicated circuits. An FM radio's demodulator is soldered in; it cannot become a Wi-Fi receiver.

A software-defined radio (SDR) keeps only the parts that must be analogue — amplifiers, filters, converters — and turns the signal into numbers as early as possible. What the radio is then depends on what you do with the numbers.

Three words you now own

ADC
Analogue-to-Digital Converter. Measures a voltage many times a second and produces a number each time. The doorway in.
DAC
Digital-to-Analogue Converter. Turns a stream of numbers back into a voltage. The doorway out.
Sample
One of those numbers — one measurement of the signal at one instant.

Every SDR has the same shape: antenna → analogue parts → ADC → numbers → something that processes them → and back out. This course is about that "something".

Why put your own logic in there at all

Often a PC is the right place. But samples arrive at up to 61.44 million pairs per second, and shipping them costs bandwidth and uncontrolled delay. Anything that must react in microseconds, shrink the data first, or be exactly timed belongs where the samples are: the FPGA.

What an FPGA is, and why it is not a processor

The single mental shift that makes everything else make sense.

A processor — the CPU in your laptop — is fixed circuitry that reads instructions one after another. It is fast because each step is quick, but it does one thing at a time.

An FPGA — Field-Programmable Gate Array — is a large field of tiny, blank logic elements plus a switching network between them. "Programming" it means configuring what circuit it physically becomes, not giving it instructions.

What the field is made of

LUT
Look-Up Table. A tiny memory, typically 6 inputs wide, that can be filled in to produce any logical function of those inputs. This board's chip has 53,200 of them.
Flip-flop
A one-bit memory that captures its input at a precise moment and holds it. Anything that remembers a value between clock ticks is built from these.
DSP slice
A hard-wired multiplier-and-adder. Multiplication is expensive in LUTs, so the chip includes 220 ready-made ones. Filters live on these.
Block RAM
Small dedicated memories for buffering data on-chip.

The consequence: everything happens at once

Describe ten filters and you get ten filters, all running simultaneously; an eleventh uses more chip, not more time. That suits radio: fixed hardware handles every one of 61 million samples per second as it passes, with predictable timing.

The beginner's mistake

Writing Verilog as if it were software. A line of Verilog is not a step after the previous line — it is circuitry that exists at the same time as every other piece. Two always blocks are two independent lumps of hardware, both live on every clock edge. If you catch yourself thinking "and then it does…", ask what the circuit looks like.

The price you pay

  • It is slower per operation: tens to a few hundred MHz. It wins by parallelism.
  • The build is slow. Synthesis, placement and routing take twenty minutes to an hour on this board, which is why simulation matters so much (lesson 10).
  • Resources are finite and visible. You will run out of DSP slices or fail timing, and have to make real trade-offs.

The Fishball7020, part by part

Two computers, one radio chip, and the connection between them.

The main chip is a Xilinx XC7Z020-CLG400, a Zynq: a processor and an FPGA on the same die.

PS
Processing System — two ARM Cortex-A9 cores. Fixed silicon, where Linux boots, the IIO daemon runs and your Python sits.
PL
Programmable Logic — the FPGA fabric of lesson 1: 53,200 LUTs, 220 DSP slices. What Verilog describes and a bitstream configures.
AXI
The bus that connects them. When Linux writes a register and your fabric logic sees it change, AXI carried that write.

The radio chip is an Analog Devices AD9361: a complete transceiver covering 70 MHz to 6 GHz, with two receivers and two transmitters — amplifiers, mixers, filters, ADCs and DACs. A fast digital interface connects it to the fabric, and the Linux driver configures it over SPI, a simple serial control bus.

Balun

Balanced to unbalanced. A coaxial cable and an SMA connector are unbalanced: one conductor carries the signal, the shield is ground. The AD9361's radio pins are balanced (differential): each port is a pair, TX1A_P and TX1A_N, carrying equal and opposite swings. A balun translates between the two; here, a tiny transformer.

Differential pairs reject noise that lands on both wires equally. Baluns are also fairly narrowband: the board covers only what its baluns pass, which can only be measured (lesson 42).

This board has power amplifiers, and most Pluto advice does not

Out: plan for roughly +19 dBm flat out. That is the self-test's capped estimate, never put on a power meter: an order of magnitude, not a specification.

In: the receive input's absolute maximum is about +2.5 dBm, roughly 16 dB below that, so a bare cable from a transmit port to a receive port destroys the receiver. Fit at least 20 dB of attenuation in any TX→RX loopback, and measure through exactly 20 dB — a bigger pad lets the board's own internal leakage into your result. Before raising output, record that you have looked at the port: ./devkit tx-guard affirm 0 (TX1A) or 1 (TX2A).

Clocks, and the one that is the radio's accuracy

Six oscillators, but only the 40 MHz reference feeding the AD9361 matters for radio work. Every frequency is derived from it, so its error is the radio's error — a reference 10 ppm high puts 900 MHz 9 kHz off.

Its pin 1, on a net named XTAL_VTC, is on JP5 pin 15; the schematic calls that pin OE, so whether it tunes the oscillator or switches it off is unconfirmed: check before driving it. EXT_CLK on a U.FL is connected to nothing as shipped: fitting the empty R109 joins it to the AD9361's reference, to feed a GPS-locked or shared reference in (or this board's 40 MHz out), and R110 joins it to the FPGA. Without soldering, ./devkit clock corrects the reference in software. TX_LO and RX_LO are on U.FL too, for running two boards coherently.

How to tell this board from the ones the internet writes about

Run iio_attr -S. FISH Ball PlutoSDR Rev.A (Z7020-AD9361) is this board. Z7010 means half the fabric; AD9363 (the ADALM-PLUTO's chip) is specified to 325 MHz–3.8 GHz and 20 MHz bandwidth. Either way this firmware will not fit unchanged.

Board vocabulary

Bitstream
The file that configures the PL — about 4 MB. It lives inside BOOT.bin on the SD card and is loaded at every power-on.
FSBL
First Stage Boot Loader. The first code that runs; it loads the bitstream and then the bootloader.
Device tree
A data file describing the hardware to Linux — which chips exist, at which addresses, with which settings. Linux does not discover any of this; it is told.
2R2T
"Two receive, two transmit" — the AD9361 mode this board runs in. It has consequences in lesson 14.

Two things the fabric cannot reach

The USER LED sits on a PS pin (MIO 0). MIO pins are not wired into the fabric, so your HDL cannot blink it; it is a software job.

Most header pins are unknown territory. Four are verified and safe to drive: JP5 pins 7, 9, 11 and 13. Driving an arbitrary pin that turns out to be an input, or tied elsewhere, can damage the board.

The toolchain and the build system

Programs with confusing names, some of them optional. Here is which one does what.

The commands you will actually type

terminalShell
# run from: the repo root
./devkit doctor     # can this machine build? answers in a second, not at minute 40
./devkit setup --target factory     # fetch upstream source and apply this repo's patches
./devkit sim        # simulate the custom HDL          (~1 second)
./devkit build --target factory     # everything                       (45-90 min)
./devkit build --target factory --hdl-only   # just the FPGA part       (~20 min)
./devkit verify --target factory    # is the build sane? checks timing, compression, files
./devkit flash --target factory     # copy onto the running board over the network

The other target: --target modern

Everything above builds the factory firmware (firmware/): the vendor's 5.15 kernel and a Buildroot root filesystem held in RAM. The modern target (firmware-modern/) is ADI's Linux 6.12 and a full Debian 13 (armhf) system on the card's ext4 partition. Modern is the default: the same commands build it without --target factory (or with --target modern, which says so explicitly). DEVKIT_TARGET=factory in your environment makes factory the default again.

terminalShell
# run from: the repo root
./devkit setup --target modern     # about 0.6 GB, not 6.8: no vendor kernel, no Buildroot
./devkit container build --target modern --xsa "$(./firmware-modern/fetch-pinned-xsa.sh)"
./devkit verify --target modern    # reads BOOT.bin's parts back out and checks them
./devkit build --target modern --rootfs-only   # the Debian system, on the host (~10 min)
sudo ./devkit write-card --target modern /dev/sdX   # the Debian root cannot be flashed over the network
  • An XSA is the FPGA design in one file: the bitstream plus a description of the hardware around it. The modern target never runs Vivado, so --xsa is required. A release's XSA is that release's design, so the tool will not guess: fetch-pinned-xsa.sh downloads the XSA of the factory release named in firmware-modern/factory-xsa.pin (v1.7 today) and refuses it unless its sha256 matches the one written there.
  • BOOT.bin is the first file the chip reads at power-on: FSBL, bitstream and U-Boot (which loads Linux). Given the same XSA, the modern target's FSBL and bitstream are byte-for-byte the factory ones.
  • On Windows, a downloaded release needs no clone at all: double-click write-card.cmd in the folder holding its files. Windows cannot make the Linux partition, so the script builds it itself, as ext3 (ext2 plus a journal), which the kernel mounts with its ext4 driver. Step by step: writing the card on Windows.
  • A cross-compiler runs on your PC but produces code for the board's ARM chip. If your desktop has no ARM Linux one, container provides it. The Debian root is the exception: it builds in a container of its own, so run that step on the host, not inside container.

The number that shapes your whole workflow

Simulation takes one second; a build takes twenty minutes. At 1200:1, every question you can answer in the simulator, you answer there (lesson 10).

Four refusals worth knowing about

Every ./devkit step is a readable script; what it adds is order and refusal, because several build mistakes are silent.

It checks before the hour. doctor tests compilers, headers, disk and board in a second, instead of a missing gmp.h failing forty minutes in.

It refuses an unpatched source tree. The build stamps which patches were applied. Add one and forget to re-run setup, and it stops rather than building the old design.

It verifies a copy before swapping it in. flash backs the SD card up, checksums the new file on the board, then moves it into place, keeping the old one. A bad BOOT.bin means a board that will not boot, and recovery then needs a card reader.

It will not use DFU, and you never should. The USB recovery path has no BOOT.bin target, so it cannot deliver an FPGA change — and on this board it has bricked units.

The one that catches everybody: a changed design that does not get built

The build script reuses an existing Vivado project, and the project is where the block design and filter coefficients live. Change either, leave the project in place, and the build succeeds with the old ones — a bitstream without your change.

Delete the project directory before any change to the block design or a .coe file. verify prints the DSP count and which coefficient file is in use, so you can see the change landed.

Vivado in a container

Vivado 2022.2 (pinned: a different Vivado makes a different bitstream) supports Ubuntu 18.04, 20.04 and 22.04 and nothing newer; elsewhere neither it nor its installer starts. The container supplies Ubuntu 22.04 on your own kernel.

terminalShell

Reaching the board, and changing its address

The board answers on 192.168.2.1 over USB and asks for an address over Ethernet: from your router, or from your PC when a cable runs straight between them. How you change either depends on which rootfs you run.

Words used here

DHCP — a router handing out addresses automatically. Static address — one you fix yourself. Default route, or gateway — where a device sends traffic for destinations off its own network; without one it reaches only its neighbours. U-Boot — the small program that runs before Linux and loads it. QSPI flash — a flash chip soldered to the board, separate from the SD card. mDNS — a device announcing its own name on the local network, so fishball.local resolves with no server.

  • It does not survive a fresh card. ./devkit write-card gives you the default back; put the change in firmware-modern/debian/overlay/etc/network/interfaces to have it on every card.
  • The MAC still comes from U-Boot. Keep the shipped pre-up ip link set dev $IFACE address "$(fw_printenv -n ethaddr)" line, or the random-MAC problem below comes back.

On the modern firmware the login prints what the board runs: the build of its boot files and of its Debian root (in red if they differ), the FPGA design, its addresses, whether both transmitters are muted, and its temperatures. Read it every time; it is the quickest check there is.

Stop typing the password

The board ships with a published root password. One command replaces it with a key:

On Debian that is one line, and a typo in it means a card reader: systemd-logind is masked on purpose (it saturates PID 1), so there is no systemctl reboot, and no console without the FTDI DEBUG cable. Prove key login works first.

On Buildroot: where the addresses actually live

Not on the SD card. They live in the U-Boot environment, a 128 KB block in QSPI flash. At every boot S40network reads it and generates the Linux network configuration.

terminalShell
# run on the board
cat /etc/fw_env.config
#   /dev/mtd1    0x0000    0x20000    0x20000
cat /proc/mtd | grep mtd1
#   mtd1: 00020000 00010000 "qspi-uboot-env"

So editing /etc/network/interfaces lasts until the next reboot. And the variables have defaults compiled into the script, so fw_printenv ipaddr answering "ipaddr" not defined does not mean no USB address — the script fell back to 192.168.2.1.

The switch that chooses DHCP or static

There is no "mode" setting: the script branches on whether ipaddr_eth has a value.

/etc/init.d/S40network, trimmedShell
if [ -n "$ETH_IPADDR" ]; then
        echo "iface eth0 inet static"   >> $IFAC
        echo "\taddress $ETH_IPADDR"    >> $IFAC
        echo "\tnetmask $ETH_NETMASK"   >> $IFAC
else
        echo "iface eth0 inet dhcp"     >> $IFAC
fi

So "back to DHCP" means deleting the variable, which fw_setenv with no value does:

terminalShell
# run on the board
fw_setenv ipaddr_eth 192.168.1.50    # a fixed address on your router's network
fw_setenv netmask_eth 255.255.255.0
fw_printenv ipaddr_eth netmask_eth   # read it back BEFORE rebooting
reboot

fw_setenv ipaddr_eth                 # no value = delete = back to DHCP
fw_setenv netmask_eth
reboot

The script also reads ipaddr (the board's USB address, default 192.168.2.1), ipaddr_host (the one address its USB DHCP server hands your PC, default 192.168.2.10), netmask, hostname (also the mDNS name) and ssid_wlan/pwd_wlan for a USB Wi-Fi dongle.

The file that looks like it works

The SD card's uEnv.txt is plain text and already contains lines like ipaddr=192.168.2.1. Editing them changes nothing in Linux. U-Boot loads uEnv.txt with env import into its environment in RAM; no saveenv runs in the SD boot path, so those values are gone before Linux starts, and Linux's fw_printenv reads /dev/mtd1, which was never touched.

Why this one fools everybody

"I edited uEnv.txt and it still works" passes either way: the script's built-in default is the same 192.168.2.1. To make a change stick from U-Boot, use its serial console — setenv ipaddr_eth …, saveenv, boot — which writes QSPI, like fw_setenv.

The route that needs no shell: config.txt

Over USB the board also appears as a small drive holding config.txt. Edit it, set reset = 1 under [ACTIONS], save, and eject the drive — the eject is the trigger. It writes the values through fw_setenv and leaves SUCCESS_ENV_UPDATE on the drive, or FAILED_INVALID_UBOOT_ENV if the write failed.

The section heading is wrong for this board

ipaddr_eth and netmask_eth sit under [USB_ETHERNET], but on the Fishball7020 they configure the RJ45 gigabit socket. The name is inherited from the ADALM-Pluto, whose only eth0 was a USB Ethernet dongle.

What static mode leaves out

The static branch writes no gateway line, and nothing writes /etc/resolv.conf. A statically addressed Buildroot board has no default route and no DNS. Measured at 192.168.129.200:

terminalShell
# run on the board
ip route
#   192.168.2.0/24     dev usb0 scope link  src 192.168.2.1
#   192.168.128.0/23   dev eth0 scope link  src 192.168.129.200
cat /etc/resolv.conf     # No such file or directory
ping -c1 8.8.8.8         # fails: no route

libiio on the same subnet still works; ntpd, git and name lookups fail silently. For a fixed address and internet, use a DHCP reservation on your router. Failing that, add what static mode omits to /mnt/jffs2/autorun.sh, the persistent partition that runs at every boot on Buildroot (nothing runs it on Debian).

Two different names, and why the router shows neither

A router listing the board as 26:ae:c6:de:ce:e0 shows two faults. Routers take the name from the DHCP request (option 12), and stock udhcpc sends none (mDNS is a separate name). And the MAC is not stable: the device tree has no local-mac-address, so the driver improvises:

macb e000b000.ethernet: invalid hw address, using random

What a random MAC per boot actually costs you

Every reboot the router sees a new device, so no DHCP reservation is possible. Two consecutive boots took 192.168.129.139 then .140 — easy to misread as "DHCP working".

Verilog from nothing

Your first module

You need about a fifth of Verilog, and this lesson is most of it.

A module is the unit of Verilog: a box with wires going in and out, and a description of what is inside.

counter.vVerilog
// Everything after two slashes is a comment.
module counter (
  input  wire        clk,     // one bit, coming in
  input  wire        rst,     // one bit, coming in
  output reg  [31:0] count    // 32 bits, going out
);

  always @(posedge clk) begin
    if (rst)
      count <= 32'd0;
    else
      count <= count + 32'd1;
  end

endmodule

Reading it line by line

  • module counter ( … ); — declares the box and lists its wires; endmodule closes it.
  • input / output — which way each wire points.
  • [31:0] — a bundle of 32 wires, 31 down to 0, bit 0 least significant: a vector.
  • always @(posedge clk) — "whenever clk goes from low to high, do the following". posedge is the rising edge.
  • <= — an assignment. Lesson 6 explains why it is this arrow and not =.
  • 32'd0 — a literal: 32 bits wide, d for decimal, value 0. You will also see 'h (hex) and 'b (binary).

Read it as hardware, not instructions: a 32-bit register count, with an adder computing count + 1 and a selector choosing that or zero by rst; on every rising edge the register captures the selector's output. About 32 flip-flops and a handful of LUTs, existing all at once.

wire versus reg

wire
A connection. Something else drives it continuously. Use for anything you assign with assign, and for module inputs.
reg
Anything you assign inside an always block. Misleadingly named: it becomes a flip-flop only if assigned on a clock edge; in a combinational block it is just a wire.
5

Clocks, registers and reset

Why digital hardware has a heartbeat, and what it is for.

A clock alternates high and low forever at a fixed rate. It carries no data; its job is to say now.

Logic takes time to settle — for a few nanoseconds an adder's output is meaningless. The clock divides time into slices: within each the logic settles, and at the edge every flip-flop captures the settled value at once.

This is what "timing" means

Timing analysis is one question: does every signal settle between one clock edge and the next? If the longest path takes longer than a period, the flip-flop at its end captures a half-finished value. Vivado reports the margin as slack (lesson 21). A pipeline register fixes it by cutting one long path into two shorter ones.

Reset

At power-on flip-flops hold arbitrary values; reset forces them to known ones.

Reset only what needs it: the reset must reach every flip-flop that uses it within one clock period, so over-resetting costs fabric and timing. A counter needs reset; a pipeline register overwritten within two cycles does not.

The clock on this board

The datapath clock, l_clk, is recovered from the AD9361's own data clock. It is not a fixed frequency: it scales with the sample rate, which software can change underneath your logic. Never assume a number of clock cycles per second.

6

The two assignments, and why it matters

The commonest cause of designs that simulate correctly and fail in hardware.

The rule, no exceptions

Use non-blocking <= inside clocked blocks (always @(posedge clk)).
Use blocking = inside combinational blocks (always @(*)).

Check yourself: which of these makes three flip-flops, and which makes one?

b <= a; c <= b; makes three — a, b and c each get a register, and c receives the old b, so a value takes two clock ticks to travel the chain.

b = a; c = b; makes one: the assignments happen in order, so c receives the new value and the intermediate register collapses away.

Why the rule exists

In a shift register all flip-flops capture simultaneously, so c gets the old b.

a shift register, correctVerilog
always @(posedge clk) begin
  b <= a;
  c <= b;      // gets the OLD b - three separate flip-flops
end
the same thing, wrongVerilog
always @(posedge clk) begin
  b = a;
  c = b;       // gets the NEW b - collapses to a single flip-flop
end

Non-blocking assignments read every right-hand side first, then update all left-hand sides together — exactly what flip-flops do on an edge. Blocking assignments run in written order: right for a chain of gates, wrong for registers. Mix them and simulation and synthesis disagree: the testbench passes and the board misbehaves.

7

Combinational logic, and the latch trap

Logic with no memory: outputs follow inputs continuously.

two formsVerilog
// continuous assignment - permanent wiring
assign sum   = a + b;
assign isbig = (a > b);          // a comparator
assign pick  = sel ? a : b;      // a multiplexer ("mux")

// block form, for anything needing if/case
always @(*) begin
  case (sel)
    2'b00:   out = a;
    2'b01:   out = b;
    2'b10:   out = c;
    default: out = 16'd0;    // ALWAYS write a default
  endcase
end

@(*) means "re-evaluate whenever any input changes", as gates do.

Inferred latches

If a combinational block fails to assign a signal on some path, the synthesiser must make the signal remember its previous value — so it builds a latch, a memory element that is not clocked.

Latches are almost never intended: hard to time, and they behave differently in simulation and hardware. Write a default in every case and an else on every if — or assign every output a safe value at the top of the block.

8

Numbers: width, signedness, overflow

Radio samples are signed and awkwardly sized. Verilog will silently do the wrong thing if you let it.

Width is not checked

Assign a 16-bit expression to a 12-bit target and Verilog silently discards the top four bits. This board moves constantly between 12-bit samples and 16-bit words, so be explicit about widths.

Signed means saying so

IQ samples are two's complement signed numbers: the top bit means negative. A plain reg [15:0] is unsigned as far as Verilog is concerned, so a > b gives wrong answers whenever negatives are involved — and half your samples are negative.

signed arithmeticVerilog
wire signed [11:0] adc;        // 12 bits from the converter
wire signed [15:0] wide;

// widening a signed number means REPLICATING the sign bit,
// not padding with zeros
assign wide = {{4{adc[11]}}, adc};

// {a, b}   concatenates
// {4{x}}   repeats x four times

Overflow, and what to do about it

Adding two 16-bit numbers needs 17 bits; multiplying needs 32. Kept in 16 bits, a large sum wraps around — a big positive becomes a big negative: a violent click and broad spectral splatter. Either grow the width, or saturate — clamp at the maximum. Saturation is usually right for signals: a clipped peak does far less damage than an inverted one.

The sizes on this board

Receive
The ADC gives 12 bits, signed, sign-extended into a 16-bit container. Full scale is ±2047, not ±32767.
Transmit
The DAC takes the top 12 bits of your 16-bit word and discards the bottom four. Scale your transmit samples to the full 16 bits — scaling them to ±2047 transmits 24 dB too quietly.

Fixed point: the arithmetic the fabric actually does

There is no floating point in the fabric: every DSP block manipulates integers pretending to be fractions.

What "fixed point" means

A floating-point number carries its own scale (an exponent), which costs hardware. The fabric uses plain integers and you remember where the binary point is; it never moves, hence fixed point.

The usual notation is Qm.n: m integer bits, n fractional bits, plus a sign bit. A signed 16-bit word holding values between −1 and +1 is Q0.15: one sign bit and fifteen fractional bits.

FormatRangeStepUsed for
Q0.15 (16-bit)−1 … +0.999971/32768Normalised samples, filter coefficients
Q1.14−2 … +1.999941/16384After a gain of up to 2
Q15.16 (32-bit)−32768 … +327671/65536Accumulators

The number that governs everything: 6.02N + 1.76

An ideal N-bit converter has a best possible signal-to-noise ratio of

the fundamental limitdB
SNR = 6.02 × N + 1.76   dB

Each extra bit buys about 6 dB. For the AD9361's 12-bit converters that is 74 dB — the ceiling.

This is where the decimation gain comes from

That 74 dB is spread across the whole sampled bandwidth. Filter down to a fraction of it and you keep the signal but discard most of the noise — process gain, 10·log₁₀(D) (lesson 27). Divide by 6.02 to get "extra bits": decimating by 307 gives 24.9 dB, 4.1 bits, taking a 12-bit converter to about 16 bits in that channel.

Scaling: the mistake that costs 40 dB

Fixed point has no automatic gain: use a small part of the range and you get a small part of the dynamic range, permanently.

Two ways to under-scale on this board

On transmit. The DAC takes bits [15:4]. Scale to ±2047, as if they were receive samples, and you use only its bottom bits. Measured on this hardware: amplitude 32767 gives 24 dB more output than 2047 — the factor of 16 the alignment predicts. Scale to ±32767.

In your own arithmetic. A signal at a tenth of full scale through a filter chain loses about 20 dB you never get back.

Growth

A 129-tap filter accumulating products needs even more width than a multiply. Grow the width and round back down at the end, or saturate (lesson 8) — never let it wrap silently, which is what Verilog does by default.

saturating addVerilog
wire signed [16:0] sum = a + b;          // 17 bits: cannot overflow
wire signed [15:0] out =
  (sum >  17'sd32767) ?  16'sd32767 :      // clamp high
  (sum < -17'sd32768) ? -16'sd32768 :      // clamp low
                        sum[15:0];

Rounding versus truncation

Truncating low bits always biases downwards, which over a long filter becomes a DC offset. Rounding — add half a least-significant bit before shifting — costs one adder and removes it.

Dither: deliberately adding noise to get a better answer

Patterned rounding error lands on discrete frequencies as spurs. A tiny random offset before rounding (dither) breaks the pattern: the floor rises slightly, the worst spur falls.

A measurement trap I walked into on this board

Quantisation error is noise-like only if uncorrelated with the signal. A tone at a simple fraction of the sample rate, such as fs/8, concentrates the error into harmonics and makes spur-free dynamic range look far worse than the hardware deserves. A full-rate sweep here at exactly fs/8 reported SFDR of 42–48 dB — pessimistic, because of the tone. Use an awkward frequency, such as a prime number of hertz.

Check yourself: your filter output is 32 bits wide and the DAC wants 16. Which 16 do you take?

Not the bottom 16 (the fractional part: you would keep the noise and lose the signal), and not blindly the top 16. Work out where the binary point sits after the multiply-accumulate, choose the window covering your signal range with headroom, round, and saturate. Then simulate with a full-scale input, the case that overflows.

The radio's datapath

What an IQ sample actually is

Every number on this board comes in pairs. Here is why.

Sample a radio wave and get 0.5: is it rising or falling? One number gives amplitude at an instant and nothing about the phase — where much of the information lives.

So SDRs take two measurements at each instant, from the same signal, using reference oscillators a quarter-cycle (90°) apart:

I
In-phase — measured against a cosine reference.
Q
Quadrature — measured against a sine reference, 90° later.

As a point on a plane — I across, Q up — the distance from the origin is the amplitude and the angle is the phase. A steady tone traces a circle; its speed of rotation is its frequency offset from the tuned centre.

Why this makes negative frequency meaningful

With I and Q a signal 1 MHz above the tuned frequency rotates one way and one 1 MHz below the other; a single real stream cannot tell them apart. That is why a spectrum here runs from negative to positive frequency either side of centre.

How it is carried here

Each sample is two signed 16-bit integers (12-bit values sign-extended on receive), interleaved I, Q, I, Q: four bytes per sample, so 5 MSPS is 20 MB/s in each direction.

Two more terms you will meet

Complex baseband
This I/Q representation, centred on zero: the carrier removed, the signal's shape around it left.
Mixing
Multiplying by an oscillator to shift a signal in frequency — from the antenna's gigahertz down to baseband, or any shift you do in the fabric (lesson 28).

Sample rate, Nyquist and aliasing

The theory you cannot skip: violating it produces signals that are not there.

The sample rate is how many samples per second you take. This board goes from 2.083 to 61.44 million samples per second (MSPS).

The Nyquist theorem, for complex I/Q sampling: rate f faithfully represents a bandwidth f, centred on the tuned frequency, from −f/2 to +f/2. At 5 MSPS you see 2.5 MHz either side of centre. (A radio sampling one real stream gets only half that — the form most textbooks state first.)

Aliasing: why a signal can appear where it is not

Sampling only glances at the signal at evenly spaced instants; nothing between is recorded. So two different sine waves can pass through exactly the same points:

A signal too fast for your sample rate does not go missing or announce itself: it is recorded as a slower one, indistinguishable from a genuine signal there. This is aliasing — the same effect that makes film wagon wheels turn backwards.

Where a given signal lands

Check yourself: at 20 MSPS, where does a signal 12 MHz above centre appear?

The window is ±10 MHz; 12 − 20 = −8 MHz, 8 MHz below centre. Try it in the calculator, then try 28 MHz, which lands in the same place.

The only real defence: filter before you sample

Once sampled, the alias is the data, so the fix must happen while the signal is still analogue. That is rf_bandwidth: a filter inside the AD9361, ahead of its converters.

It wraps, it does not mirror — and that catches people out

With real sampling an alias reflects about the band edge. With complex I/Q sampling the band is a circle: a tone pushed off the top reappears at the bottom.

When you decimate — keep one sample in every D (lesson 27) — there are two Nyquist limits, and the smaller governs.

“Filter at the new Nyquist” is wrong: a real filter has a transition band. 16 MSPS decimated by 8 (2 MSPS out, Nyquist 1 MHz), 193-tap Hamming low-pass cut off at exactly 1 MHz:

How to catch an alias in the field

Suspect a peak? Change the sample rate and look again. A genuine signal stays put; an alias jumps or vanishes.

What this costs you in practice

At 61.44 MSPS each direction is 245 MB/s. Measured on this board, capture alone runs at the full 61.44 MSPS over gigabit Ethernet with a handful of dropped samples per two million. The walls are elsewhere: simultaneous transmit and receive streaming starve above about 5 MSPS, and a modest CPU cannot do much with 245 MB/s in real time.

Transmit is fragile earlier than you would guess. Fed from the host at only 3.072 MSPS (12 MB/s), it took one starve mute inside ten seconds; with the client on the board, none. What matters is the play-out time each buffer holds: 256 K samples at 3.072 MSPS is 85 ms, so a longer stall empties the DAC, and 250 ms of empty mutes the transmitter. Larger buffers buy slack.

Streaming without a break is set by the board's CPU, not the network: 12 MS/s at 16 bits and 20 MS/s at 8 bits per receiver, measured on a direct gigabit cable, which is barely more than over Wi-Fi. Anything faster has to be captured on the board first (lesson 3A has the numbers).

If you only care about a 200 kHz signal, why sample 56 MHz at all? See lesson 27.

The block design: the map, and what it quietly assumes

You are joining a design that already works, so the useful question is where you can cut in, and what will bite you.

It is a script, not a drawing

Vivado shows the block design as a diagram, but here it is generated, every build, by firmware/src/hdl/projects/pluto/system_bd.tcl. So your change is a reviewable diff; clicking in the diagram edits a project that will be regenerated; and the project is a treacherous cache — the build reuses an existing project rather than re-running the script (lesson 21).

How software reaches into the fabric at all

Through memory-mapped I/O. Most of the processor's addresses are ordinary memory (the DDR chips), but some are wired to hardware: writing one changes a register in a fabric block, and reading one asks the hardware what it currently is. Nothing in the number says which kind it is; the address map and the device tree tell you. The block design's job is to decide which addresses land on which blocks.

Clocks, valid strobes, and the 2R2T trap

Three facts about this board that otherwise cost a day each.

First: what a clock domain is

A clock domain is the set of flip-flops driven by one clock. Inside one domain signals move in lockstep; trouble starts when a signal crosses between unrelated clocks, whose edges drift past each other (lesson 22). This board has three:

ClockWhere it comes fromRateWhat it drives

sys_200m_clkFCLK_CLK1 from the processor 200 MHz, fixed Only a timing reference for the link to the radio chip. Not for your logic.

Two terms from that table

Recovered clock
The AD9361 sends a clock alongside its data; the fabric runs on it rather than generating its own, so it changes with the sample rate.
LVDS
Low-Voltage Differential Signalling — the chip-to-fabric link's electrical standard: each bit is the difference between two wires. Fast, noise-resistant, and few wires carrying many bits in turn.

l_clk is not a fixed frequency

Around 4 MHz at the AD9361's slowest rate; 61.44 MHz at 30.72 MSPS. No "wait 1000 cycles for one millisecond": design for the top end, and count events, not time.

Second: what a strobe is, and why a clock is not one

A strobe is a brief pulse that says "now", as distinct from a level:

A levelA strobe
Says"this is the state of things""this event is happening, right now"

So why not just use the clock?

Because the clock always ticks, and the data is not always new. Here a tick may carry nothing you want because:

  • The channels take turns. In 2R2T, half the ticks belong to the other channel.
  • A filter is decimating. With the ÷8 filter on, seven ticks in eight produce no output.
  • The data ran out. On transmit, the hardware substitutes zeros.

Between samples the wires hold the last value, so logic acting on every edge processes the same sample repeatedly. Average 1000 samples on every edge in 2R2T and you add each sample twice, averaging over half the intended window — a plausible, wrong number, which is what strobe bugs produce.

the pattern, every timeVerilog
always @(posedge clk) begin      // clk decides WHEN you may act
  if (valid_in)                  // the strobe decides WHETHER you should
    accumulator <= accumulator + sample_in;
end

The clock grants permission to act; the strobe supplies the reason. Every block in the datapath works this way, which is why they chain.

The strobes you will meet, and what each is announcing

"The data on these wires is real this cycle." The default meaning of "strobe".

"Store this, now." cpack/fifo_wr_en is one.
Read enable
"Give me the next one." A request, not the data arriving.
Underflow
"I had nothing to give, so this is a substitute."
Check yourself: why does a strobe last exactly one cycle, rather than staying high while data is good?

It marks an event, and events are counted: high for three cycles, downstream logic acts three times on one sample. A lasting condition is a level, like flag; mixing the two gives counts off by a factor.

Third: 2R2T, where clock edges stop meaning samples

This board runs the AD9361 with two receivers and two transmitters — "2R2T" — over one physical link, so the channels are time-multiplexed: one clock period carries a channel-0 sample, the next a channel-1 sample, and so on.

A clock edge is not a sample: there are twice as many edges as samples of one channel.

What goes wrong, concretely

A counter incremented every edge, its bottom bit on a header pin as a scope trigger, toggles at twice the intended rate; every pattern comes out doubled, and still looks plausible. Gate on the valid strobe:

the 2R2T ruleVerilog
if (valid_in) count <= count + 1'b1;   // counts SAMPLES
// count <= count + 1'b1;              // counts CLOCK EDGES - twice as fast
Check yourself: at 15 MSPS, how fast does l_clk run, and how many edges per channel-0 sample?

30 MHz, twice the sample rate, so two edges per channel-0 sample: one carrying channel 0, the next channel 1.

The strobes on this board

A request is not an arrival

The packer registers its output, so the word requested by fifo_rd_en appears the following cycle. Capture on fifo_rd_en and you latch the previous sample, silently one behind. Use fifo_rd_valid | fifo_rd_underflow: it goes high with the data, covering real samples and substituted zeros.

The packers, and the filter ADI gives only channel 0

Two blocks sit between the radio and memory. One is wired lopsidedly, and that will quietly ruin your second receiver if nobody warns you.

Why anything sits there at all

The radio produces one stream per receiver; memory wants one wide, regular stream. The packers reconcile the two.

The receive one is cpack ("channel pack"); the transmit one is tx_upack ("unpack"), and does the reverse.

Three words used below

First In, First Out — a small on-chip queue; one side pushes at its own pace, the other pulls at another.

What "packing" actually does

With one receiver enabled, each 64-bit word holds four 16-bit samples of it; with two, two samples of each, interleaved. Software picks the channels when it opens a buffer, the packer rearranges on the fly, and memory is densely filled. One channel gives I, Q, I, Q…; two give I0, Q0, I1, Q1… — no header, no channel tag.

Then how does your program know which sample is which?

By something it already set. Before opening a buffer an application enables channels, and the hardware packs them in a fixed order: their scan index, ascending.

Getting the mask wrong is silent

Enable four channels but de-interleave as though there were two, and every other sample pair lands in the wrong stream — no error, just signals that look like noise.

At the wire-protocol level the mask is a fixed-width hex field: 00000003 enables channels 0 and 1, 0000000F all four. Writing 3 instead of 00000003 fails with -22 EINVAL and no explanation.

Check yourself: you enable only channel 1. What does a 64-bit word contain?

Four 16-bit samples of channel 1 — two complete I/Q pairs — and nothing of channel 0. A disabled channel costs no memory and no bandwidth.

Between the radio interface and cpack, channel 0 passes through rx_fir_decimator; channel 1 goes straight past. That hierarchy contains:

  • One filter per stream — two here, for I and Q (four once channel 1 is routed through it too).
  • A clock-domain crossing for the on/off signal (lesson 22's problem, already solved here).
  • Bypass multiplexers, one per stream, all following a single active bit. The filters are always present in the fabric; what moves at runtime is the bypass.

What is it for? The chip has a floor

Why a decimating filter in the fabric, when the AD9361 has filters of its own? Because the AD9361 cannot go slow enough:

2,083,333 samples per second is a hard floor in the silicon, so the narrowest slice this radio delivers alone is about 2.1 MHz — 700 times wider than SSB voice (3 kHz), 10 times an FM broadcast channel or GSM carrier (200 kHz). The fabric decimator removes that floor. Measured on this board, converter as slow as it goes:

It extends the radio's usable range downward, past the silicon's floor. That also explains:

  • Why the factor is fixed at eight, and the driver offers only {1, 8}: it is one specific extension of the rate range, not a general resampler.
  • Why there is a matching ×8 interpolator on transmit: the floor applies in both directions.
  • Why both share one coefficient file: one generic anti-alias design for a factor of eight, used in opposite directions.

And why the channel-1 problem was invisible to whoever designed it

This block design serves a family of boards, many of them 1R1T — one receiver, one transmitter — where channel 0 is the only channel. It is a defect only on a 2R2T board.

Why it is a filter and a ÷8, never just a ÷8

Keeping every eighth sample is undersampling (lesson 12). One in eight of 61.44 MSPS is 7.68 MSPS, a ±3.84 MHz window, and everything outside it folds in: eight slices summed, so any transmitter in the other seven lands on your signal and the noise is roughly eight times higher.

The FIR filter empties those seven slices before the samples are discarded: an anti-alias filter.

Downsampling is keeping one sample in D, and aliases; decimation is filtering first, then keeping one in D.

Where the free dynamic range comes from

Decimating by D buys 10·log₁₀(D) dB of signal-to-noise (lesson 27) because the filter discards the noise in the slices you are not keeping. Remove the filter and the gain vanishes: processing gain and anti-alias filter are the same fact.

So what is in the .coe file?

One tap value per line, 129 of them. That list is the filter, and its stopband depth sets how much aliasing survives: at 80 dB whatever folds in arrives 80 dB weaker.

Check yourself: why can the AD9361's own analogue filter not do this job for you?

It protects the converter's rate (rf_bandwidth), and the converter still runs at full speed. Every point where the rate drops needs its own anti-alias filter.

How samples reach memory, and back

The full journey in both directions: packer, DMA engine, a dedicated port into the DDR chips, your program's buffer. Every address and setting here is this board's.

At 61.44 MSPS each direction is 245 MB/s, more than a 666 MHz ARM core can copy. A DMA engine (Direct Memory Access) moves the data instead and tells the processor when a whole buffer is done.

The pieces, named

The board's main memory: two Micron chips totalling 1 GB, on the processor side.
AXI
The on-chip bus standard: AXI-Stream is a plain "word, valid, ready" flow; AXI-MM (memory-mapped) carries addresses.
HP port
A High-Performance port into the memory controller that the fabric uses without the CPU. This board wires two.
Descriptor
"Move this many bytes, to or from this address": written by software, executed by the engine.

Both are ADI's axi_dmac, one per direction, 64 bits wide on the fabric side — exactly the width the packers produce and consume.

Each DMA straddles two clock domains

The fabric side runs on l_clk, which moves with the sample rate (lesson 14); the memory side on sys_cpu_clk, a fixed 100 MHz. The DMA is itself a well-tested clock-domain crossing.

Receive, step by step

  1. The AD9361 sends samples over the LVDS link. axi_ad9361 recovers the clock, deserialises, sign-extends 12 bits to 16, applies DC-offset correction.
  2. The samples pass through rx_fir_decimator (lesson 15) — both channels in a default build; only channel 0 on ADI's wiring.
  3. cpack interleaves the enabled channels into 64-bit words, one per pulse of fifo_wr_en.
  4. The receive DMA's input FIFO absorbs them, and the engine bursts them across S_AXI_HP1 into DDR, at addresses from a descriptor.
  5. When a buffer is full, the engine raises interrupt ps-13; the driver marks it ready and hands the next one to the hardware.
  6. Your program's read() — or iio_readdev, or pyadi-iio — returns the completed buffer. No sample was ever copied by the CPU.

SYNC_TRANSFER_START makes a capture begin on a clean packer boundary; without it your first samples could be the tail of a word, with the channel order shifted.

Transmit, step by step

  1. Your program writes samples into a buffer; the driver hands the DMA a descriptor pointing at it in DDR.
  2. The engine reads from DDR across S_AXI_HP2 and presents 64-bit words to tx_upack as an AXI-Stream, one per pulse of fifo_rd_en.
  3. tx_upack splits each word back into per-channel 16-bit samples.
  4. axi_ad9361 takes bits [15:4] of each — the DAC is 12 bits — and sends them over the LVDS link. Bits [3:0] are discarded, which is what the sample-locked GPIO feature reuses.
  5. Interrupt ps-12 tells the driver a buffer has been consumed, so it can queue the next.

Underflow looks like a broken radio

If software does not refill fast enough, the transmit engine substitutes zeros and raises fifo_rd_underflow. Measured on this board, that happens above about 5 MSPS when transmit and receive stream continuously at once — and it looks like terrible modulation quality, not missing data, so it is easy to misdiagnose.

The receive equivalent is overflow: software has not drained the previous buffer, so samples are lost.

CYCLIC 1, and why a crashed program can keep transmitting

The transmit engine can repeat a buffer forever — how you transmit at the full 61.44 MSPS with the host feeding nothing. The repetition lives in hardware, so your program exiting, crashing or being killed does not stop it.

An ordinary, non-cyclic transmit used to behave the same way, as a defect: killing a local transmit client left the radio live, measured 12.6 dB above the muted floor. This firmware now mutes when the converter stops being fed: no data for 250 ms and the attenuator goes to maximum, measured at 0.27 s from the kill. Read the timeout at /sys/bus/iio/devices/iio:device2/tx_starve_timeout_ms, and keep a different one across reboots with fw_setenv tx_starve_ms. The first block of a new buffer gets longer, in proportion to its size, because it is uploaded after the buffer is switched on: a 4.4 MB pattern used to be muted before it had even arrived.

Cyclic is exempt from that: a healthy cyclic stream sends nothing after its one buffer, so a kill -9 on it looks like normal operation. Make stopping it explicit and verified. A separate bound, tx_cyclic_timeout_ms, is armed at 60 s at every boot on the modern (Debian) target; on the factory target it is off unless you set fw_setenv tx_cyclic_bound.

Two things about that watchdog that are easy to get wrong. First, it does not re-arm. Once it has fired, data arriving again does not change the driver's mind — only opening a fresh buffer does. So after a starve-mute, writing a gain raises the attenuator and nothing puts it back, not even stopping the stream. What keeps the port quiet then is the powered-down TX LO, so never read hardwaregain alone and conclude anything; read out_altvoltage1_TX_LO_powerdown beside it.

Second: mute before you close the buffer, not after. When a stream stops, the driver caches whatever attenuation it finds, applies maximum, and gives the cached value to the next buffer anybody opens. Measured on the bench: a board reading a fully muted −89.75 dB came up at −61.5 dB the moment a buffer was opened, 28 dB nobody asked for, because the previous run closed before muting. Several tools in this repo had it backwards, including the one that runs in CI.

So opening a transmit buffer is not a neutral act: the enable can bring the oscillator up and lift an attenuator. A program that means to stay silent cannot write −89.75 and trust it; it must read both attenuators back after the enable and stop if either moved.

The worked example, line by line

A real module from this repo, trimmed to its core. Twenty lines of logic that teach four habits at once.

What it does. The AD9361's DAC is 12 bits, but you hand it 16-bit samples. It takes the top 12 and throws the bottom four away — literally, in ADI's HDL: dac_data_out <= dma_data[15:4]. This module catches those four bits and puts them on four header pins, in lockstep with the sample that carried them. It costs nothing in signal quality, because the bits never reached the DAC.

tx_gpio_bitmap.vVerilog
module tx_gpio_bitmap (
  input  wire       clk,        // l_clk
  input  wire       rst,
  input  wire [3:0] sample_in,  // the discarded low nibble
  input  wire       valid_in,   // fifo_rd_valid | fifo_rd_underflow
  input  wire       flag,       // enable, from an AXI register
  input  wire [3:0] gpio_in,    // what the PS wants when disabled
  output wire [3:0] pin_o
);

  // (1) 'flag' is written in the AXI clock domain and read here in
  //     l_clk. Two flip-flops make that safe. See lesson 22.
  reg flag_meta, flag_sync;
  always @(posedge clk) begin
    flag_meta <= flag;
    flag_sync <= flag_meta;
  end

  // (2) capture on the VALID strobe, never on the clock: in 2R2T a
  //     sample arrives only every second l_clk edge.
  reg [3:0] held;
  always @(posedge clk) begin
    if (rst)            held <= 4'd0;
    else if (valid_in)  held <= sample_in;
  end

  // (3) our nibble, or the processor's GPIO
  assign pin_o = flag_sync ? held : gpio_in;

endmodule

The four habits

  1. Synchronise anything arriving from another clock. flag is written by Linux. Sampling it directly risks metastability. Two flip-flops cost almost nothing.
  2. Gate on valid, never on the clock. The 2R2T trap from lesson 14.
  3. Reset only what needs it. held does; the synchroniser flops do not. Over-resetting wastes fabric and strains timing.
  4. Keep the bypass clean. With flag clear the pins behave exactly as before. A feature you cannot fully switch off is one people will not switch on.

Try it

Change the module so a second flag bit puts a free-running counter on the pins instead of the sample nibble. You now have a divided sample clock on a header pin — a scope trigger with a fixed, known relationship to the transmitted RF, which no software timing can give you.

Step 1 — write the RTL with the packager in mind

Vivado infers interfaces from signal names: name ports the way it expects and a bus connects in one line; otherwise you get loose pins forever. A receive-path scaler in this board's conventions:

library/util_rxscale/util_rxscale.vVerilog
module util_rxscale #(
  parameter DATA_WIDTH = 16
) (
  input  wire                    clk,        // l_clk - the recovered clock
  input  wire                    resetn,

  // ---- the ADI datapath convention, in ----
  input  wire                    valid_in,   // one pulse per sample
  input  wire                    enable_in,  // channel is switched on (a level, not a strobe)
  input  wire [DATA_WIDTH-1:0] data_in,

  // ---- and out ----
  output reg                     valid_out,
  output wire                    enable_out,
  output reg  [DATA_WIDTH-1:0] data_out,

  input  wire [7:0]              shift       // runtime control, see step 6
);
  assign enable_out = enable_in;      // pass the level straight through

  wire signed [DATA_WIDTH-1:0] s = data_in;

  always @(posedge clk) begin
    if (!resetn) begin
      valid_out <= 1'b0;
      data_out  <= {DATA_WIDTH{1'b0}};
    end else begin
      valid_out <= valid_in;           // SAME latency as the data - one cycle
      data_out  <= s >>> shift;        // arithmetic shift: keeps the sign
    end
  end
endmodule

The contract you are signing when you cut into this datapath

  1. Delay valid by exactly as much as data. One extra register on the data and every sample downstream carries its neighbour's strobe.
  2. enable is a level, not a strobe — "this channel is on". Do not pipeline it or treat it as a per-sample qualifier.
  3. Do not change the rate unless you mean to. One valid_in, one valid_out. Producing fewer is a decimator, with the consequences below.
  4. Everything is on l_clk. Not sys_cpu_clk. A value from the processor side crosses domains — lesson 22.

Crossing clock domains

Bugs that pass simulation and timing, then fail on hardware occasionally.

Linux writes your enable bit in the AXI clock domain; your datapath runs on l_clk, which follows the sample rate. The two have no fixed relationship.

A flip-flop needs its input stable for a short window around the clock edge. If the input changes then, it can enter metastability: neither 0 nor 1, resolving after an unpredictable delay, and different downstream logic may resolve it differently.

two-flop synchroniserVerilog
reg meta, sync;
always @(posedge dest_clk) begin
  meta <= src_signal;   // this one may go metastable
  sync <= meta;         // by now it has had a full cycle to settle
end
// use 'sync' everywhere. Never use 'meta'.

One bit only

Fine for slowly-changing control bits; wrong for a multi-bit value. Each bit settles independently, so you can read a number that never existed. Multi-bit crossings need a handshake or an asynchronous FIFO.

Telling the tools it is deliberate

Synthesis does not know the crossing is intentional, so say so in the XDC:

system_constr.xdcXDC
set_max_delay -datapath_only \
  -from [get_cells .../flag_reg] \
  -to   [get_cells .../flag_meta_reg] 4.000

A real bug from this repo's history

set_max_delay -datapath_only requires -from. Without it Vivado silently drops the constraint, with nothing in the log. That left the bit-map flag's crossing unconstrained through several releases. Patch 0009 fixed it, and CI now fails the build if any -datapath_only line lacks its -from.

The frequency domain, and what a spectrum really is

What a spectrum is, and the three settings that decide whether to trust one.

One signal, two descriptions

A signal can be described by what it does over time (a list of samples) or by which frequencies it is made of. They are two complete accounts of the same thing, and converting between them loses nothing.

The conversion is the Fourier transform; on a computer, the DFT (Discrete Fourier Transform), computed by the fast algorithm called the FFT. "4096-point FFT" means 4096 samples in one go.

The intuition

To ask "how much 1 MHz is in this signal?", multiply it by a 1 MHz reference wave and sum the products: if 1 MHz is there the sum grows, if not it cancels to near zero. Do that for every frequency and you have a spectrum.

What the picture is actually showing

An FFT of N samples returns N bins, each the energy in one narrow slice of frequency. Plotted in decibels, that is the spectrum. Because this board samples I and Q (lesson 11), the bins run from −fs/2 to +fs/2 around the tuned frequency; negative offsets mean "below the local oscillator".

Three settings decide what you see: the sample rate sets the span (2.083 to 61.44 MSPS here), the FFT length N sets the resolution, fs / N, and the window sets how far a strong signal smears over its neighbours.

Try it — what can this FFT actually resolve?

Why a window is needed

The FFT assumes your chunk repeats forever. Unless it holds a whole number of cycles, the end does not meet the start, and that step adds broadband energy, spectral leakage: skirts either side of a tone that bury anything small nearby. The fix is to multiply the chunk by a shape tapering to zero at both ends, a window. Hann is the default; Blackman-Harris leaks less but is wider; rectangular means none. The cost is a tone roughly 1.5× wider, almost always worth it.

The trade you cannot escape

Finer resolution needs a longer FFT, so a longer capture, so less ability to see fast change. Frequency and time resolution trade directly: at 61.44 MSPS a 4096-point FFT covers 66.7 µs and 15 kHz per bin; at 2.083 MSPS, 1.97 ms and 509 Hz.

Processing gain, and why a noise floor is not a number

Noise spreads across every bin; a tone lands in one. Double the FFT length and each bin collects half the noise, so the tone rises 3 dB above the floor with nothing about the signal changed.

So a noise floor means nothing without its FFT length; two measurements minutes apart can differ by 10 dB through this alone.

Check yourself: at 61.44 MSPS with a 4096-point FFT, can you separate two signals 10 kHz apart?

No. Resolution is 61.44 MHz / 4096 = 15 kHz per bin, so both fall in one bin. Either lengthen the FFT (65 536 points gives 937 Hz) or, usually better, drop the sample rate: at 2.083 MSPS the same FFT gives 509 Hz bins and the capture is thirty times smaller.

Where noise actually comes from

Three sources stack up, in this order of importance on this board:

  2. The receiver's own noise, its noise figure (NF): how many dB worse than that ideal the hardware is. The AD9361 is roughly 3–6 dB, depending on gain and band.
  3. Quantisation noise, the 6.02N + 1.76 dB limit from lesson 9; usually the smallest of the three here, which is the point of 12 bits.

Narrow your view, gain SNR

The noise floor depends on bandwidth. Halve the bandwidth and you halve the noise power: 3 dB better signal-to-noise, with the signal unchanged. The FFT-length effect of lesson 24, this, and decimation's process gain (lesson 27) are one principle: noise is spread out, signal is not, so narrowing your view favours the signal. "Listen to less spectrum" is the first thing to try on a weak signal.

SNR is a number and a bandwidth

"The SNR is 30 dB" is incomplete without the bandwidth it was measured in. A tone in a 15 kHz FFT bin and the same tone across 5 MHz differ by 10·log₁₀(5 000 000 / 15 000) = 25 dB, with nothing about the radio changed.

Tone SNR in an FFT bin, what tools/selftest/sdr_selftest.py reports, is flattering because the bin is narrow. SNR across the occupied bandwidth is what a demodulator experiences and predicts whether a link works. Noise figure belongs to the hardware alone. Quote the first where the second was meant and a link with "70 dB of SNR" fails to decode.

DSP in the fabric

Filters, from first principles

Almost everything you build in the fabric is a filter or contains one.

A filter passes some frequencies and rejects others. Digitally, each output sample is a weighted sum of the most recent inputs.

a 4-tap FIR, conceptuallyVerilog
out[n] = c0*in[n] + c1*in[n-1] + c2*in[n-2] + c3*in[n-3];

That is a FIR filter, Finite Impulse Response: feed it a single spike and the output stops after a finite number of samples (four, here). The weights c0..c3 are taps or coefficients, and they are the entire design.

Why this filters anything at all

Averaging neighbours smooths, cancelling rapid wiggles: a low-pass filter. Subtracting neighbours emphasises change: a high-pass. Every other response is a more careful choice of weights.

What it costs in fabric

N taps need N multiplications per output. Multiplies are expensive in LUTs, which is why the chip has 220 dedicated DSP slices. A naive 100-tap filter would take 100 of them; the next lesson shows why that estimate is usually far too pessimistic.

Fixed point, and the two ways it goes wrong

The fabric has no floating point; coefficients are scaled integers. Overflow: a 16×16 multiply needs 32 bits and an accumulator more; truncate carelessly and a loud signal wraps into noise. Quantisation: rounding the coefficients fills in the stopband, so a filter designed for 80 dB of rejection may deliver 50 with 12-bit coefficients.

Decimation, and why taps are nearly free

The most useful trick in fabric DSP, and the reason this repo's channelizer is affordable.

Decimation produces one output for every D inputs, lowering the sample rate by D. Filter first, or the discarded bandwidth aliases back in (lesson 12).

The trick: one output every D inputs gives the hardware D input-sample periods per output, so one multiplier is reused D times. A 128-tap filter decimating by 8 needs roughly 16 multipliers, not 128. Taps are nearly free, so this repo's coefficient generator designs a simple Kaiser-windowed sinc rather than chase the roughly 30% tap saving of an equiripple design.

Why sample at 61 MSPS only to throw most of it away?

Capture 56 MHz, decimate by 307, keep a 200 kHz channel: 99.7% discarded. The AD9361 can sample at 200 kSPS directly, and for a fixed signal with no strong neighbours that is simpler and uses no fabric. Wide-then-decimate earns its complexity four ways.

1. About four extra bits of dynamic range

The converter's quantisation noise is roughly fixed in total power and spread across the whole sampled bandwidth. Filter to a narrow slice and you keep all the signal but only a fraction of the noise: a gain of 10·log₁₀(D) dB.

2. A digital filter is far sharper than the analogue one

The analogue filter is a few poles with a corner that drifts with temperature. A 321-tap FIR gives 80 dB of stopband rejection, a transition band a few percent wide and exactly linear phase: with a strong transmitter 300 kHz away, only it saves you.

3. You can retune without touching the radio

Retuning the AD9361's local oscillator takes time, disturbs its calibration and breaks phase continuity. Retuning a fabric mixer (lesson 28) is one register write, phase preserved: the only workable approach for direction finding or coherent multi-channel work.

4. One capture, many channels

One capture can be split into as many narrow, simultaneous, phase-coherent channels as you have fabric for. That is a channelizer, and tuning cannot do it.

Mixers, oscillators and CORDIC

How to move a signal in frequency without touching the radio's tuning.

Multiplying a signal by a complex exponential shifts it in frequency; that is all a mixer is. Multiply by ej2πft and the spectrum slides up by f; a negative f slides it down.

The sine and cosine come from a numerically-controlled oscillator (NCO). The classic one is a phase accumulator: a counter that adds a fixed step every sample, ramping and wrapping like an angle, fed into a sine lookup table.

phase accumulatorVerilog
reg [31:0] phase;
always @(posedge clk)
  if (valid) phase <= phase + step;   // step sets the frequency

// frequency = step / 2^32 * sample_rate
// the accumulator wraps naturally, which is exactly what an angle does

A lookup table costs block RAM that grows with precision. CORDIC computes sine, cosine and rotation with only shifts and adds, one iteration per bit of precision, which pipelines well. For frequency shifting, rectangular-to-polar conversion or magnitude and phase, it is usually the right answer on an FPGA.

Pulse shaping, and why root-raised-cosine

The shape given to each symbol decides how much spectrum you occupy and whether symbols smear into each other.

The obvious idea, and why it fails

Send lesson 29's QPSK symbols at 1 Msym/s by holding each steady for a microsecond: a train of rectangles. But a rectangle in time is a sinc in frequency, with skirts falling as 1/f that never end. A signal that ought to need about 1 MHz still radiates measurably 20 MHz away. Sharp edges in time are wide in frequency.

Two demands that fight

Be narrow in frequency, so you do not splatter over neighbours, and clean in time, so neighbouring symbols' tails contribute nothing at each sampling instant. Smearing between symbols is ISI, intersymbol interference, and it closes the eye as surely as noise. Narrowing a pulse in frequency lengthens it in time.

Nyquist's trick: zero at the sampling instants

A pulse need not be short, only zero at every other symbol's sampling instant. Such pulses are Nyquist pulses, and the usual one is the raised cosine, with one knob, the roll-off β, between 0 and 1:

βBandwidth neededTime-domain tailsIn practice
0exactly Rs, the minimumring forever, decay as 1/tunbuildable; brutally timing-sensitive
0.221.22 × Rsmodest3G/UMTS
0.351.35 × Rsshortcommon default; this repository's test waveforms
12 × Rsvery shortwasteful, very timing-tolerant

β buys timing tolerance with bandwidth. Occupied bandwidth = (1 + β) × symbol rate is the formula to keep.

Why root raised cosine, and why it is split in half

The Nyquist condition applies end to end: transmitter, channel and receiver together. So the transmitter gets a root raised cosine (RRC) and the receiver an identical one; the two square roots multiply back to the raised cosine.

The split is strictly better

The receiver's RRC is also the matched filter, shaped exactly like the transmitted pulse, which lesson 32 shows maximises SNR at the decision instant: zero ISI and the best receiver SNR. Every digital standard does it.

How you actually build one

Pulse shaping is upsampling followed by an FIR filter, lesson 27's decimator run backwards:

  1. Take one complex symbol per symbol period.
  2. Insert sps−1 zeros after each: the upsample step. sps is samples per symbol, usually 4.
  3. Run the result through the RRC filter, whose taps fill in the zeros with the pulse shape.

The filter's span is how many symbol periods of tail it keeps. Span s at sps samples per symbol is s×sps+1 taps; this repository's test waveforms use span 12 at 4 samples per symbol, 49 taps.

tools/modulation-gallery/waveforms.py — the shaper, in outlinePython
up = np.zeros(nsym*sps, dtype=complex)
up[::sps] = syms                  # one symbol, then sps-1 zeros
h  = rrc(0.35, 4, 12)             # alpha, sps, span: 49 taps
x  = np.convolve(up, h)           # the shaped waveform (the file shapes circularly)

In the fabric: a strobe once per symbol, a multiply-accumulate chain, and lesson 27's polyphase reuse so no multiplier is wasted on the zeros.

Synchronisation: finding the signal inside the samples

A receiver does not get symbols. It gets a stream of numbers with the symbols hidden at unknown times, phase and orientation. Recovering them is a whole discipline; here is enough to avoid the classic traps.

Three unknowns, in order

UnknownWhy it existsWhat it does if ignored
Timing — when is a symbol? Your sample clock and the transmitter's are unrelated, and the cable adds delay. You sample between symbols and get a smeared constellation.
Carrier phase — which way is up? The two oscillators have no agreed phase reference. The constellation is rotated by an arbitrary angle.
Carrier frequency — is it drifting? Two independent oscillators are never exactly equal. The constellation spins.

On a loopback, one of the three is free

Transmit and receive here share one 40 MHz reference, so their synthesisers produce equal frequencies. Measured here (QPSK raised to the fourth power shows a tone at four times the offset): 0.0 Hz.

Timing recovery

Matched-filter the stream, then decide which sample within each symbol period is the right one. The classic detectors are Gardner and Müller & Mueller, feedback loops that nudge the sampling instant until an error measure settles.

For an offline capture, try every candidate phase and pick the best. For a constant-envelope modulation like QPSK, the correct instant is the one with the least spread in magnitude:

brute-force timing searchPython
# z is the matched-filtered stream, sps samples per symbol
best = None
for phase in range(sps * OVERSAMPLE):
    s = z[phase::sps * OVERSAMPLE]
    # 1/kurtosis: 1.0 means perfectly constant magnitude
    q = mean(abs(s)**2)**2 / mean(abs(s)**4)
    if q > best: best, best_phase = q, phase

That number is diagnostic in itself. A clean lock reads 1.000; near 0.5 the symbols look Gaussian — the data is corrupted, not merely mistimed. That is how the DMA-starvation problem on this board was first identified.

Phase ambiguity, and the bug it causes

QPSK's four points are symmetric under 90° rotation, so the standard way to remove an unknown phase is to raise the symbols to the fourth power — which cancels the data — take the angle, and divide by four. The result can still be rotated by any multiple of 90°. That residue is phase ambiguity; real systems resolve it with a known preamble or by encoding data in phase changes.

The 45° version, which cost real time here — twice

Estimating phase as angle(mean(s⁴))/4 leaves the constellation at 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, but a QPSK reference lattice conventionally sits at 45°, 135°, 225°, 315°. Every symbol is a half-quadrant off, and the error vector magnitude reads about 76% — which looks exactly like a broken transmitter. It was hit twice here, on two estimators, and both times the radio was blamed first.

The check: run your demodulator against the clean file you transmitted. If it does not read close to 0%, the bug is yours.

Check yourself: your EVM is 76% and the spectrum looks perfect. What do you suspect?

The analysis, not the radio. A clean spectrum means the signal arrived with its shape intact; 76% is the value a 45° lattice mismatch gives (|ej45° − 1| ≈ 0.765). Genuine corruption degrades the spectrum too.

Correlation, matched filters, and finding the packet at all

Lesson 31 assumed you knew a signal was there. This is the step before: deciding that something is there, and where it starts. It is also the operation this board's fabric is best at.

Correlation, in one sentence

Slide a known template along the received samples, and at each offset multiply the two point by point and add up the result. Where the template lines up with a real copy of itself, every product is positive and the sum shoots up; elsewhere it stays small. That is correlation — the Fourier transform of lesson 24 is the same operation with a sine wave as the template.

Why the answer is a filter, not a search

Correlating against a template is identical to running an FIR filter whose taps are that template, time-reversed and complex-conjugated. So you write the filter from lesson 26 and load different coefficients. That filter is the matched filter: of every possible filter, it gives the highest signal-to-noise ratio at the moment the template lines up — which is why lesson 30's receiver-side RRC is not arbitrary.

Where the gain comes from, and how much

Add N lined-up samples of signal and the amplitudes grow N times; add N samples of noise and they grow only as √N. The ratio improves by √N in amplitude — 10·log₁₀(N) dB in power. A 64-sample preamble buys 18 dB; a 1024-sample one 30 dB. That is how a signal below the noise floor is found: you accumulate it. It is the same trade as FFT length (lesson 24), noise bandwidth (lesson 25) and process gain (lesson 27).

Try it — how long must the preamble be?

What makes a good preamble

The template is usually a preamble, a known sequence at the front of every packet. It must look like itself at zero offset and like nothing at every other offset. That property is its autocorrelation, and the ideal is a single sharp spike — a thumbtack.

Frequency offset eats your correlation length

Coherent accumulation assumes the phase stays put across the window. A frequency offset turns it by 2π·Δf·N/fs over N samples; past half a turn the second half of the window cancels the first, and a longer preamble makes detection worse. Keep the rotation under about a tenth of a turn: N < fs / (10·Δf). Untuned AD9361s can sit tens of kilohertz apart: at 5 MSPS and 10 kHz that is about 50 samples. Beyond that, correlate in shorter blocks and add magnitudes, losing several dB.

A correlation peak is not a detection

A raw peak grows with input level, so a strong interferer trips a fixed threshold before your packet does. Divide the correlator output by the energy in the same window: the normalised value runs 0 to 1 and lets a threshold survive a 40 dB change in level. Then set the threshold from the false-alarm rate you can live with, not from one capture that worked.

OFDM, and why this board finds it hard

Wi-Fi, LTE, 5G, DVB-T and DAB all use it. It is also the one waveform this board measurably struggles with, and the reason why is the most useful thing in this lesson.

The problem it solves

A signal arriving by two paths — direct, and bounced off a wall — arrives twice, slightly apart. That is multipath, and the delay between the first and last meaningful echo is the delay spread: tens to hundreds of nanoseconds indoors, microseconds outdoors. The echoes add in phase at some frequencies and cancel at others, so the channel becomes a comb with notches — a frequency-selective channel.

A single-carrier receiver needs an equaliser spanning the delay spread: at 20 Msym/s and 1 µs, a continuously retrained 20-tap adaptive filter.

The OFDM bargain

Instead of one carrier at 20 Msym/s, send 64 carriers at 312.5 ksym/s each. Each is so narrow that the channel across it is a single complex number, so equalisation collapses to one complex multiply per subcarrier. Everything else in OFDM is machinery to make that legal.

The FFT is the modulator

You do not build 64 oscillators. Write your 64 symbols into the bins of a frequency-domain array and take an inverse FFT: out comes the time-domain waveform with all 64 carriers on it. The receiver's forward FFT reads them back.

one OFDM symbol, 802.11a parametersPython
X      = np.zeros(64, dtype=complex)
X[idx] = qpsk_symbols          # 52 used bins; DC and the edges left empty
x      = np.fft.ifft(X) * np.sqrt(64)
out    = np.r_[x[-16:], x]     # cyclic prefix: the last 16 copied to the front

A 64-point FFT, 52 data subcarriers, a 16-sample cyclic prefix. The DC bin is empty because a direct-conversion receiver has its LO leakage there (lesson 11); the edges are a guard band. ofdm() in tools/modulation-gallery/waveforms.py uses the same 52 carriers on a 128-point FFT with a 32-sample prefix.

Orthogonality, and why the spacing is not negotiable

The subcarriers overlap but do not interfere, because each fits a whole number of cycles into the symbol period, so over that period every other subcarrier integrates to zero. That is orthogonality, and it forces Δf = 1 / Tsymbol. A timing error, frequency offset or sampling-clock mismatch breaks it and the subcarriers leak into each other — ICI (inter-carrier interference). OFDM is far more sensitive to frequency offset than single-carrier.

The cyclic prefix: the bit that looks like waste

Copy the last 16 samples of the symbol to the front and send 80 instead of 64 — 20 % of capacity. It buys two things:

  1. Echoes land in the guard, not in the next symbol. If the delay spread is shorter than the prefix, the tail of symbol n falls inside the prefix of symbol n+1, which the receiver discards.
  2. The channel becomes a multiply. The prefix makes the channel's linear convolution behave like a circular convolution over the FFT window, which is a plain multiplication in frequency. That makes the one-tap equaliser exact.

So CP duration > delay spread, and anything longer is capacity given away.

Try it — size an OFDM signal for this board

Equalisation, in the only place it is easy

Scatter a few pilot subcarriers of known value through the grid, or send one known symbol first. The channel at that subcarrier is H = Y / X; interpolate across the pilots, then recover every data subcarrier with X̂ = Y / H. One complex division per subcarrier per symbol. Lesson 34 is what a single-carrier receiver must do instead.

Why OFDM always arrives with error-correcting code attached

A channel notch does not degrade everything a little — it wipes out a few subcarriers, and dividing by a near-zero H amplifies their noise enormously. The fix is forward error correction plus interleaving: scramble the coded bits across subcarriers so a wiped-out group becomes scattered single-bit errors the code repairs. Hence COFDM — coded OFDM. Coding typically buys 4–6 dB, which a link budget (lesson 41) may count on; the uncoded test waveform here is not what any real system ships.

Measured on this board, and the honest explanation

At 61.44 MSPS over the 20 dB cable loopback, the test waveforms in docs/modulation-and-throughput.md give QPSK 2.17 % EVM, 16-QAM 2.24 % — and OFDM between 13 % and 51 %, run to run. That looks like a broken implementation. It is not:

Equalisation: undoing a channel that is not flat

A single-carrier receiver cannot make the channel one number per subcarrier, so it has to undo the channel directly — with a filter that designs itself.

What a channel does to your signal

Multipath (lesson 33) adds several delayed copies together. Written as samples, that is a convolution — the FIR filter of lesson 26, with coefficients nobody chose:

what arrivesthe channel, as a filter
y[n] = x[n]*h[0] + x[n-1]*h[1] + x[n-2]*h[2] + ... + noise
       |                |
       what you sent    the echo, half as strong, one sample late

The channel is an unwanted FIR filter, so the fix is another FIR filter that undoes it — plus three complications.

Three words before we go on

Tap
One coefficient of a filter. A "7-tap equaliser" remembers seven samples.
Pre-cursor / post-cursor
Energy from a symbol that lands before its own sampling instant, and after it. A symbol-spaced equaliser needs taps on both sides.
Convergence
An adaptive filter starts wrong and improves. Convergence is how long that takes, measured in symbols.

Complication one: you cannot simply invert it

The obvious equaliser is 1/H, the zero-forcing equaliser. In the notches H is nearly zero and 1/H enormous: it removes the ISI and multiplies the noise by a hundred. The standard answer is MMSE — minimum mean-square error — which minimises total error and so leaves a little ISI in deep notches rather than amplifying noise. Every practical equaliser is MMSE.

Complication two: you do not know the channel, and it changes

So the filter finds its own coefficients while running. The workhorse is LMS — least mean squares — in three lines:

LMS, per received samplethe whole algorithm
y = dot(w, x)          # filter: w is the taps, x the last N samples
e = d - y              # error: d is what the symbol should have been
w = w + mu * e * conj(x)   # nudge every tap toward reducing that error

After a few hundred symbols the taps settle into the MMSE solution on their own.

µ, the step size
Too large and the taps diverge; too small and the equaliser is still converging when the packet ends. Normalised LMS divides µ by the input power, so one choice works across a 40 dB range of levels — almost always what you want.
RLS
Recursive least squares: converges in tens of symbols, at N² arithmetic per sample.

The decision-feedback equaliser

A DFE uses a feed-forward filter for the pre-cursor ISI and a second filter that subtracts post-cursor ISI using symbols already decided — clean, noiseless points — so it cancels ISI without amplifying noise. The price is error propagation: one wrong decision helps produce the next. Excellent at high SNR, fragile at low SNR.

Channel coding: buying decibels with redundancy

Lesson 33 said coding is worth 4 to 6 dB and lesson 41 spends them. This is where they come from and what they cost.

The idea, and the exchange rate

Send more bits than the message needs, arranged so the extra ones let the receiver find and repair wrong bits. The code rate is R = k/n: rate 1/2 sends every message bit as 2 transmitted bits. In return you get coding gain — the reduction in SNR needed for the same error rate. A rate-1/2, constraint-length-7 convolutional code with soft decisions buys about 5 dB, for a few thousand LUTs rather than a bigger amplifier and a licence.

Why there is a limit, and roughly where it is

Shannon's capacity formula says a channel of bandwidth B and signal-to-noise ratio S/N carries at most C = B · log₂(1 + S/N) bits per second, error-free, with a good enough code. Below about −1.6 dB of energy per bit against noise density, no code works; turbo and LDPC codes get within roughly 1 dB of that floor.

Soft decisions, and the free 2 dB most people throw away

Rounding the demodulator's output to the nearest symbol and handing the decoder a bit is a hard decision. Handing it the distance as well — "probably a 1, but only just" — is a soft decision, usually a log-likelihood ratio (LLR). Soft decisions are worth about 2 dB, for free.

  • Your demodulator must output LLRs, not bits. A slicer in the middle of the chain throws the information away permanently.
  • LLRs need the noise level scaled correctly (measure SNR, lesson 40).

Interleaving, or the code does nothing

Every code in that table is designed against scattered errors. Real channels produce bursts — a fade, an interferer, a group of OFDM subcarriers in a notch — and 40 consecutive bad bits defeat a code that repairs 40 scattered ones easily. An interleaver writes the coded bits into a block in one order and reads them out in another, so neighbours in the codeword are far apart on the air and the burst is spread across many codewords. The code without the interleaver is close to useless.

The waterfall, and why a code can be worth nothing at all

Error rate against SNR for a coded link is flat and terrible, then falls off a cliff over about 1 dB — the waterfall. Above it the code is worth its full 5 dB; below it, slightly less than nothing, because you spent half your throughput on it. Coding does not rescue a link that is 10 dB short. It rescues one that is 3 dB short.

In the fabric

The encoder is a shift register and two XOR trees — a handful of LUTs, no DSP slices. The decoder is the work:

Viterbi, K=7
64 states, an add-compare-select per state per bit, and a traceback memory. A few thousand LUTs and a BRAM or two, easily fast enough at symbol rate.
Reed–Solomon
Finite-field arithmetic: awkward in software, natural in LUTs. Also available as IP.
LDPC
Hundreds of parallel messages iterating five to fifty times. Fits on this part only in small, slow configurations.

As always (lesson 29), put it at symbol rate behind the decimation.

Check yourself: your link is 3 dB short. Rate-1/2 coding gives 5 dB of gain — do you take it?

Usually, but rate 1/2 doubles the transmitted bits: either halve the data rate at the same bandwidth (2 dB ahead), or double the bandwidth — which raises the noise floor 3 dB (lesson 25) and occupies twice the spectrum. A rate-3/4 code (about 3.5 dB) is often the better trade.

Iterative decoding: how turbo and LDPC actually work

The mechanism behind codes within a decibel of Shannon is one idea — nodes exchanging opinions until they agree — and the three common ways to break it follow from one rule.

The currency: log-likelihood ratios

the log-likelihood ratioone number, two jobs
L(x) = ln[ P(x = 0 | y) / P(x = 1 | y) ]

  sign      the decision        positive means 0
  magnitude the confidence      0 means "no idea"

For BPSK in Gaussian noise, L = 2y/σ²: the received sample over the noise power. Gray-mapped QPSK is two independent BPSK channels. As log-ratios, independent evidence adds.

The scaling check worth writing into your test bench

For a rate-R code the channel LLRs should have variance σ²L = 8 · R · Eb/N0 and mean μL = σ²L/2 — the consistency condition, straight from L = 2y/σ². Histogram your LLRs once and check it.

LDPC: opinions on a graph

Draw the parity-check matrix as a Tanner graph: one variable node per coded bit, one check node per parity equation, an edge wherever the matrix has a 1. Every edge carries a number in both directions:

Variable node
Pools opinions: adds the channel LLR to every check node's message.
Check node
Enforces "these bits exclusive-or to zero": from how sure it is about the other bits in its equation, it says what the remaining one must be.

Iterate until the parity check passes — H·x̂ᵀ = 0 — or you run out of patience.

The one rule the whole field rests on: extrinsic information

A message sent out along an edge is computed from everything except what came in on that edge. Break that and a node hears its own opinion back as fresh evidence: confidence rises, the error stays, and the syndrome never clears — a bookkeeping bug that looks like an arithmetic one.

Turbo: two decoders, one interleaver

A turbo encoder is two small convolutional encoders seeing the same data, the second through an interleaver. Their soft-in soft-out decoders take turns, handing over only the part of their answer the other did not already know:

the handoffextrinsic information
L_in  = L_A + L_CH                 // what I was told, plus the channel
L_E   = L_out - L_in               // what I worked out that nobody told me
L(u)  = L_CH + L_E1 + deinterleave(L_E2)   // the final decision

LE is interleaved into the other decoder's prior, so it sees the errors in a different arrangement.

Polar: not iterative at all

Polar codes decode in one sweep, each decided bit removing its effect before the next — successive cancellation:

successive cancellationthe whole recursion
f(a, b) ~= sign(a) * sign(b) * min(|a|, |b|)    // a min-sum check node
g(a, b, s) = (-1)^s * a + b                     // a variable-node sum, sign-flipped
                                                // by the bits already decided

One bad early decision poisons the rest, so list decoding carries L candidate paths and lets a CRC pick the survivor — why 5G's polar codes include a CRC.

Min-sum hides a bug that sum-product exposes

Scale every input LLR by a positive constant and min-sum gives the same decisions — min and + are positively homogeneous. Min-sum does not care what your noise-variance estimate is. Sum-product does, because tanh(L/2) is nonlinear, so a wrong σ² costs several tenths of a decibel the day you switch decoders. Over-scaling also fails: LLRs that saturate a 4-to-16-bit fixed-point datapath create an error floor that looks like a bad code.

How many iterations, and what stops you going further

MathWorks' 5G NR LDPC hardware decoder defaults to 8 iterations with a maximum of 63 (a six-bit field) and stops early when the syndrome clears. High-throughput LDPC chips use 3 to 5; DVB-S2X's performance tables assume 50.

More iterations stop helping at the error floor, where the curve flattens. Three different causes:

LDPC — trapping sets
Small groups of variable nodes whose local structure traps the decoder in a stable wrong answer. Richardson (2003) showed these, not low-weight codewords, dominate LDPC floors.
Turbo — genuine minimum distance
Small minimum distance that grows only logarithmically with interleaver length, so longer blocks do not lower the floor; interleaver design does, within that bound. LTE's quadratic permutation polynomial is that lever.
Yours — implementation
A mistuned α or β, or coarse quantisation, creates an artificial floor. The one you can fix, and the first to rule out.

EXIT charts: predicting the cliff without simulating it

Plot each constituent decoder's output mutual information against its input, one with axes swapped: decoding converges if and only if the two curves do not touch, so a code can be designed to a target SNR without simulating it (ten Brink, IEEE Transactions on Communications, 2001).

Scrambling: the step that looks pointless and is not

Payloads contain long runs of zeros. Sent literally, they give a waveform with no transitions, so lesson 31's timing recovery drifts and loses lock, and a constant symbol puts a spike in the spectrum and a DC offset where this receiver already has LO leakage (lesson 11).

The smallest useful thing to build

A one-way beacon: preamble, sync word, a 1-byte length, 32 bytes of payload, a CRC-16. Generate it in Python, shape it (lesson 30), load it once with iio_writedev -c and let the hardware repeat it. Check yourself: why can a beacon run at 61.44 MSPS on this board while a two-way link struggles at 5?

A beacon's bytes never change, so cyclic mode replays one buffer with the host out of the loop — no samples cross the network once it is loaded.

A two-way link needs new bytes every frame and must react to what it hears, so every sample crosses the 31 MB/s bottleneck and every decision waits for a buffer to complete. The rate limit and the latency limit are the same constraint, and the way out of both is to move the reacting part into the fabric.

Beyond one link: protocols, routing, and what a network adds

A network means more than two nodes, more than one hop, and something above you that expects an ordered byte stream. Most of it you can inherit rather than write.

The layer model, honestly

The seven-layer OSI model (ITU-T X.200) is almost never implemented. What is deployed is the four layers of RFC 1122: application, transport, internet, link. And even those leak — RFC 3439's "Layering Considered Harmful": "multiplexing and segmentation both hide vital information that lower layers may need to optimize their performance."

What you write, and what you get for free

You write
The physical layer (lessons 29–36), framing and media access (lesson 37), and optionally link-layer retransmission.
Linux gives you
IP, ICMP, UDP, TCP, routing tables, sockets — free, the moment your program presents a network interface. The usual route is a TUN device (TUN carries IP packets, TAP Ethernet frames; a subnetwork under IP wants TUN). Your program reads packets out of it, transmits them, and writes received ones back in.

So you are building a subnetwork underneath IP. RFC 3819, "Advice for Internet Subnetwork Designers", is the most useful thing to read after this lesson.

Addressing: who, and where

A MAC address says who: flat, permanent, 48 bits, the top 24 the manufacturer's OUI. A network address says where, so prefixes can be aggregated (RFC 4632) instead of the routing table holding one entry per device.

For a small radio network, copy 802.15.4: a 16-bit short address plus a 16-bit network identifier — 65 534 usable nodes once 0xFFFF is broadcast and 0xFFFE "not assigned yet". Every byte of header is airtime.

Split a datagram into N fragments and it survives only if all of them do. Encryption without authentication is worse than it sounds

Counter-mode ciphertext (AES-CTR and similar) is malleable: flipping a ciphertext bit flips the same plaintext bit. An attacker who knows what a field says can change it without the key.

A CRC does not stop it. A CRC is linear over exclusive-or — "a general property of all CRC checksums" — so you can compute the CRC change that matches your data change. That is what killed WEP: CRCs "are only designed to protect against random bit errors, not intentional forgeries" (NIST SP 800-97 §3.2.3). Lesson 37's CRC is error detection, not integrity, and not authentication.

The nonce rule is not a guideline

For GCM, the probability of ever using the same key and IV twice "shall be no greater than 2−32" — "in practice, this requirement is almost as important as the secrecy of the key" (NIST). The counter must survive a power cut, and on this board that means flash

The most common way an embedded radio ends up insecure while appearing to work: counters reset to 0 at boot, so nonces are reused, and the product "appears to work but actually fails to secure communications" (Sastry and Wagner, 802.15.4). LoRaWAN 1.0.4 made it normative: "ABP end-devices SHALL store the frame counters persistently".

Writing flash every frame is slow and wears it, so use a lease: write a counter value well ahead of where you are, count from RAM below it, and on reboot skip past the whole leased block. You lose a few thousand values per power cycle and never reuse one.

On this board the persistent partition on the QSPI flash is /mnt/jffs2; reflashing the kernel, device tree and bitstream leaves it untouched, on either target. (On the modern target the Debian root on the SD card is writable too, but rewriting the card wipes it.)

Forward secrecy, and where LoRaWAN does not have it

Forward secrecy means compromising the long-term key does not expose earlier session keys. LoRaWAN's session keys are a deterministic AES function of the root key and two nonces, so whoever records traffic and later obtains the root key decrypts all of it — a deliberate trade for small devices, but one to notice you are making.

Spoofing, and the most instructive example in the world

The GPS civil signal is an unauthenticated broadcast: the C/A code is published, and the navigation message has only a Hamming parity code — error detection, not cryptography. Your link is the same until you add authentication: anything that receives it will believe anything that sounds like it.

Encrypted does not mean invisible

Traffic analysis infers presence, volume, timing and size "even if flows are encrypted" (RFC 6973), and on radio the transmission itself reveals that a transmitter exists and roughly where. The countermeasures — spreading, minimum power (lesson 41), directivity, traffic padding — all cost margin, throughput or energy.

Physical-layer security: what it claims, and whether to use it

Wyner's wiretap model gives a secrecy capacity when the eavesdropper's channel is worse, and channel reciprocity lets two ends derive a key from fading a third party (more than about half a wavelength away) does not see. It is not a substitute for cryptography:

  • Key generation needs the channel to change; in a static room a 256-bit key takes minutes.
  • Published active attacks on real hardware have recovered a large fraction of the key bits, and an adversary with more antennas can drive the secrecy rate to zero.
  • No standards body endorses it: 5G mandates AES, NIST requires approved random bit generators.

Legality and security are different regimes — and on this board they can conflict

Spectrum rules impose no duty to secure a link, and unlicensed operation is conditional: no claim to a frequency, interference must be accepted, and you stop when told. In the US amateur service, messages "encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning" are prohibited with narrow exceptions — so a legal ham-band link may not be legal to encrypt. Authentication does not obscure meaning and is the workable path. Check your own jurisdiction.

Three traps, all of them common

Inventing your own construction
Including combining standard primitives in your own order. Use a single standard AEAD mode where you need both encryption and authentication (NIST SP 800-175B).
Reusing a nonce across reboots
The product works perfectly while unprotected. There is no symptom.
Reusing one key in two contexts
Two independent counters under one key collide, and the XOR of two ciphertexts under one keystream breaks confidentiality. The nonce state should never be separated from the key.

Check yourself: SNR is excellent but EVM is terrible. What is wrong?

Link budgets: will it actually work over the air?

Once an antenna is involved, one calculation decides whether the link exists at all, and because every term is in decibels it is a single column of addition.

The whole calculation

the link budgetall terms in dB
  received power   = TX power + TX antenna gain - cable loss
                     - path loss + RX antenna gain      [dBm]

  noise floor      = -174 + 10*log10(bandwidth_Hz) + noise figure   [dBm]

  SNR              = received power - noise floor       [dB]
  margin           = SNR - SNR the demodulator needs    [dB]

A positive margin means it works as assumed.

Path loss, and what it really is

In free space a wave spreads over an ever-larger sphere and a receiving antenna catches an ever-smaller share. Nothing is absorbed. That is free-space path loss:

free-space path lossdB
FSPL = 32.44 + 20*log10(distance_km) + 20*log10(frequency_MHz)

Double the distance and you lose 6 dB. Double the frequency and you lose another 6 dB. The air is not more absorbent at 2.4 GHz; a fixed-gain antenna is physically smaller at the higher frequency, so it catches less of the sphere.

Free space is the optimistic case, and you are rarely in it

FSPL assumes nothing between the antennas. Real environments obey a steeper law, distance raised to an exponent n:

EnvironmentExponent nLoss per doubling of distance
Free space2.06 dB
Open outdoor, ground reflection2.5–37.5–9 dB
Indoors, same floor3–49–12 dB
Indoors, through floors4–612–18 dB

At 100 m, an exponent of 3.5 instead of 2 costs an extra 30 dB. This is why budgets that look comfortable on paper fail in a building.

Antenna gain is not amplification

What it is
A statement about shape. 6 dBi means four times the power of an isotropic radiator in the favoured direction, by radiating less everywhere else.
dBi vs dBd
dBi is referenced to an ideal isotropic radiator; dBd to a half-wave dipole. dBd + 2.15 = dBi. Datasheets mix them, because dBi is the bigger number.
The consequence
Gain is directivity, so it comes with a beamwidth.

Antennas and the RF front end: what happens after the SMA

"Transmit power" and "antenna gain" are hardware, and on this board some of that hardware is missing. No amount of Verilog fixes this part of the chain.

Fifty ohms, and why anything has an impedance at all

Once the wavelength is comparable to the cable, a signal reaching the far end meets an impedance. If it does not match the cable's own characteristic impedance, part of the wave reflects. Radio standardises on 50 Ω throughout so that it does not.

Three names for one measurement

VSWR
Voltage standing wave ratio. 1:1 is perfect, 2:1 ordinary, 3:1 poor.
Return loss
The same in decibels: how far below the forward wave the reflected one is. Bigger is better. 2:1 VSWR is 9.5 dB.
S11
The same again, as a complex number from a vector network analyser. Its phase tells you which way the mismatch is, which is what lets you design the fix.

At 2:1 about 11 % of your power comes back, only 0.5 dB. At 3:1 it is 25 %, and at 6:1 half your transmit power heads back into the amplifier.

Try it — antenna dimensions and mismatch loss

The antenna, in the three facts that matter

It is a resonant length
A quarter-wave monopole is about a quarter of the wavelength, a few percent shorter: at 900 MHz about 78 mm. The wrong length moves the impedance off 50 Ω and power reflects.
A monopole needs a ground plane
It is half a dipole; the other half is its mirror image in the ground plane. A whip on a small board with no ground plane is a worse antenna than the datasheet's.
Small is bad, unavoidably
Much smaller than a quarter wave still matches, but only over a narrow band and with more loss. Bandwidth, size and efficiency trade by physical law, which is why a small device's antenna is nearly always the weakest part of the link.

Polarisation: 20 dB that is nowhere in the link budget

Turn one of two vertical whips horizontal and, in clean line of sight, coupling drops by 20 dB or more. If either end moves, use circular polarisation at one end (3 dB always, instead of 20 dB sometimes) or diversity (lesson 46). Indoors, scattering softens the effect, so the failure is worse outdoors.

The front end, and what this board has

From the parts list in docs/hardware.md:

The AD9361's front end is wideband by design (70 MHz to 6 GHz), so nothing here protects a narrowband receiver.

Receiving: a strong signal you are not listening to still ruins your day

An FM broadcaster or nearby LTE base station arrives at the SMA at full strength whatever you tuned to. The front end sets its gain for the loudest thing present, pushing your wanted signal down too. This is desensitisation, or blocking, and it looks like a weak signal: the noise floor seems to rise for no reason. The fix is a band-pass filter at the antenna, the first accessory worth owning.

Transmitting: your harmonics leave the building too

The amplifier emits copies at twice and three times the frequency, measured on this board at −64 to −80 dBc (second) and −71 to −85 dBc (third). Into an antenna at +19 dBm, the second harmonic of 900 MHz is a real transmission at 1.8 GHz, in somebody else's band, at up to −45 dBm. This board has no transmit low-pass filter, so before anything radiates know what you may transmit, where, at what power, and at what harmonics.

Where the noise figure is decided

In a cascade, the first stage dominates. Friis's formula:

noise figure of a cascadelinear, not dB
F_total = F1 + (F2 - 1)/G1 + (F3 - 1)/(G1*G2) + ...
                     |
              divided by the first stage's gain

A low-noise amplifier with 15 dB of gain at the front makes the rest nearly irrelevant. Hence put the LNA at the antenna, not at the radio: coax with 1 dB of loss in front of the LNA costs a whole decibel of noise figure; behind it, almost nothing.

Cables, connectors, and the reason every measurement here says "exactly 20 dB"

  • Use a real attenuator. This board's receive input is rated to about +2.5 dBm and its transmitter reaches about +19 dBm. A loopback without at least 20 dB of pad destroys the receiver, once, permanently. Raising a port's output also needs a human on record: ./devkit tx-guard affirm 0 (TX1A) or 1 (TX2A) after you have looked at that port, and ./devkit selftest --loopback refuses to transmit without it.
  • Exactly 20 dB, not more. With a bigger pad the board's internal transmit-to-receive leakage (an equivalent 58 to 77 dB of isolation below 1 GHz, only 33 to 51 dB at 3 to 6 GHz) competes with the cable path, and you measure the board's crosstalk.
  • SMA connectors have a torque spec. A loose one is an intermittent mismatch that looks like a flaky radio.

The L network, and the bandwidth you do not get to choose

Two components, closed-form. Let Rsmall and Rlarge be the smaller and larger of source and load:

L-network design, completetwo elements
Q      = sqrt( R_large / R_small - 1 )

|X_series| = Q * R_small        // in series with the SMALLER resistance
|X_shunt|  = R_large / Q        // across the LARGER resistance

one of them is an inductor and the other a capacitor (opposite signs)
fractional 3 dB bandwidth = 1 / Q

Q is fixed by the resistance ratio alone, so the bandwidth is decided the moment you know what you match to what. 50 Ω to 10 Ω gives Q = 2 and about 50 % bandwidth.

The single most common RF measurement error

Calibrated at the coaxial interface, the measurement still includes your connector, launch and feed line. For return loss magnitude that is survivable. For impedance it is fatal: the fixture rotates Γ around the chart, and you design a shunt capacitor where the antenna needed a series inductor.

Fixes, in order of rigour: de-embed a measured fixture model; TRL on your own board; or at least set the port extension against a short and an open at the feed point.

VSWR 1.5 (return loss 14 dB) is a good match; VSWR 2.0 (9.5 dB) is where the matching network should be reviewed, and the usual threshold for quoting antenna bandwidth. At 2:1 the antenna still takes 88.9 % of the transmitter's power.

What is actually in the file

iio_readdev writes raw samples to stdout with no header: the packer's output from lesson 15, little-endian 16-bit signed integers, interleaved:

The same int16 means two different things on this board

Receive
The converter is 12-bit, sign-extended into an int16. Full scale is ±2047, not ±32767.
Transmit
The DAC takes the top 12 bits, so you fill the whole ±32767 range and the hardware discards the low nibble, the same nibble docs/tx-gpio-bitmap.md routes to the header pins.

Divide a receive capture by 32768 instead of 2048 and every absolute level is 24 dB too low, consistently, so nothing looks wrong. The most common way to publish a confidently incorrect dBFS figure from this board.

Two coherent receivers: what this board can do that most cannot

The shared local oscillator looked like a limitation in lesson 15. It is also this board's most interesting capability.

Both receivers run from one synthesiser, sample on one clock, and are captured on one strobe. They are therefore coherent: the phase relationship between them is stable and meaningful — one measurement from two viewpoints, not two recordings.

examples/03-coherent-receivers shows the phase on a dial whose radius is the coherence, and avoids the trap at the end of this lesson.

What the phase difference tells you

A wave arriving at an angle reaches one antenna slightly before the other. That delay is a phase difference between the channels, and from it you can compute the direction the signal came from.

direction from phasePython
# rx1, rx2: simultaneous captures from the two receivers
phase_diff = np.angle(np.mean(rx2 * np.conj(rx1)))
# d = antenna spacing, lam = wavelength
angle_of_arrival = np.arcsin(phase_diff * lam / (2 * np.pi * d))

With antennas half a wavelength apart this is unambiguous across a useful arc: the basis of direction finding, and of beamforming, where you add the two channels with a deliberate phase shift to steer sensitivity.

Coherent does not mean calibrated

Each chain has its own amplifiers, cables and gain settings, so there is a fixed phase and amplitude offset between them unrelated to the signal. On this board the two receivers differ by about 1.5 dB in sensitivity before correction. Calibrate first: feed both inputs one signal through a splitter, measure the offset, subtract it.

And do not engage the fabric decimator on stock firmware

All of this needs the two channels sample-aligned. On upstream's wiring, engaging the ÷8 decimator filters channel 0 and not channel 1, offsetting them by the filter's group delay.

A bitstream built from this repository is already fine: patch 0021 filters both channels in lockstep and is applied by default (lesson 15). The warning is for factory firmware and for a build made with STOCK_RX_FILTER=1; on those, leave the decimator bypassed.

Try it

MIMO and beamforming: what more than one antenna buys

Finding a direction from the phase between two receivers is one of three different things people mean by "multiple antennas". Know which one you are asking for.

Three things, one name

What it doesWhat it needsWhat it gives
DiversityTwo antennas see independent fades; use whichever is better, or combine themAntennas far enough apart to fade independently Up to 3 dB of gain, and — far more valuable — most of the fade margin back
BeamformingCombine with deliberate phase shifts so the array listens or shouts in one directionKnown geometry and calibrated phase 3 dB per doubling of elements, plus rejection of interference from other directions
Spatial multiplexingSend different data from each antenna at the same time on the same frequencyA rich scattering channel, and N×N antennasN times the throughput, with no extra bandwidth or power

Only the third is "MIMO" in the strict sense. All three are available here in some form, and they want opposite antenna spacings.

Why the spacing is the design decision

Diversity wants the antennas far apart — several wavelengths — so their fades are uncorrelated. Beamforming wants them close and exact — half a wavelength, so the phase difference maps to one angle. At 900 MHz that is 167 mm.

Wider than half a wavelength and several angles give the same phase difference: the array develops grating lobes. Much narrower and the phase difference shrinks into the noise. No spacing is good at both jobs.

Diversity, and the thing that actually rescues links

Selection combining
Take whichever branch is stronger. Nearly free, and most of the benefit.
Maximal ratio combining (MRC)
Co-phase the branches and add them, weighted by their own signal-to-noise. Optimal: 3 dB of plain gain with two equal branches, but worth 10 to 20 dB of fade margin, because both branches being deeply faded at once is the square of the probability that one is.

Beamforming, in the only maths you need

Two elements d apart, a wave arriving at angle θ from broadside. The far element is d·sin θ further away, so its signal is late by that much — in phase:

the whole of array processing, twicetwo elements
receive (direction finding)   phase difference = 2*pi * d * sin(theta) / lambda

transmit (steering a beam)    apply that same phase difference deliberately,
                              and the two waves add in phase toward theta
                              and cancel elsewhere

At d = λ/2 that is φ = π·sin θ: broadside gives 0°, endfire 180°. Two elements give a broad beam and one ambiguity: 30° in front of the array and 30° behind it give the same φ; a third or directional element resolves it. Scanning φ across all angles is a beamscan; MUSIC and ESPRIT do better (PySDR chapters 19 to 21).

Try it — spacing, angle and ambiguity

Spatial multiplexing, and why it is not free here

If the 2×2 channel matrix H is invertible, the receiver can solve for two streams sent at once on one frequency (802.11n onward). But H must be well-conditioned: two whips side by side in free space give a near-singular matrix whose inverse amplifies noise enormously. Multipath, everywhere else the enemy, is what makes spatial multiplexing work.

What this board genuinely has, and what it does not

  • Two receivers on one RX_LO and two transmitters on one TX_LO. The chains are coherent and share phase noise, so it cancels in the difference — unusual at this price.
  • Two is not many. Serious direction finding wants four or eight elements, which means several boards locked to one clock.
  • Nothing is calibrated. The chains differ by about 1.5 dB in receive and 0.1 to 0.25 dB in transmit on the measured unit, each with its own fixed phase offset — easily tens of degrees, and different at every frequency. Measure it with a splitter (lesson 45) and re-measure after every retune.
  • Transmit calibration drifts. The AD9361's transmit quadrature calibration varies by up to 10 dB run to run here, so a transmit beamformer needs re-calibrating far more often than a receive one.

Four elements from two boards

Two boards whose EXT_CLK inputs share one reference (a GPS-disciplined oscillator with one output per board) make a four-element line. The shared reference makes their LOs run at exactly the same frequency, and that is all it does. Each board's LO still lands at an unknown phase after every retune, and the two boards' captures start milliseconds apart, because nothing starts them together. So a calibration with a source straight ahead comes first, and each element's phase is read where it does not depend on when its capture started: at the peak of a matched filter, against a chirp that sweeps symmetrically across the LO.

The devkit's beamformer example does that through the automation server, and starts with a lock check: an element whose phase rotates against the beacon within one capture is on a different reference. Tried on one board, with TX2's phase standing in for the arrival angle, it found five directions from −60° to +60° within 0.3°. It gained nothing in SNR, though, and the reason is the first bullet above turned round: noise the two chains share cancels in their difference, and for the same reason adds up in step in their sum, where averaging cannot remove it. Array gain needs noise that differs from element to element.

Check yourself: you want diversity and direction finding from one pair of antennas. Can you?

Not well: diversity needs several wavelengths of spacing, direction finding half a wavelength or less. At a wide spacing the phase difference maps to several possible angles, so you need another constraint — a third antenna, a prior, or movement. With two chains, pick the job; if you need both, add elements rather than compromise the spacing.

Retuning across 1300 or 4000 MHz silently changes what your gain setting means

When the RX LO changes band, ad9361_load_gt rewrites all 77 rows and remaps your current index: old index to dB via the old table, then that dB looked up in the new one, clamping to the top if there is no match. An index set at 900 MHz is a different physical gain — different LNA/mixer/TIA mix, noise figure and compression point — at 2.4 GHz, and nothing reports it. Read gain back after a retune.

The 128-tap FIR, and its rules

Coefficients are signed 16-bit, written low byte then high byte through 0x061/0x062 on transmit and 0x0F1/0x0F2 on receive. The constraints, all in ad9361_load_fir_filter_coef:

  • Tap count must be a multiple of 16, and at most 128 (ntaps > 128 || ntaps % 16 is rejected).
  • The FIR does its own rate change of 1, 2 or 4; 4 is written as 3, and 0 means bypass, not ÷1.
  • At interpolation 1, transmit is capped at 64 taps. In general max_taps = (converter_rate / sample_rate) × 16: at full rate there are not enough clock cycles per sample for 128 coefficients.
  • Gain is quantised. Receive offers −12, −6, 0 or +6 dB; transmit only 0 or −6 dB.

The tap limit is where lesson 47's 2.083 MSPS comes from: libad9361 tests rate <= 25000000 / 12 — 2 083 333 Hz — and steps the chip through an intermediate 3 MSPS before toggling the FIR, because enabling 128 taps directly at the final low rate would violate it. Custom coefficients go in through the filter_fir_config attribute as a .ftr text file.

What the driver does that you did not ask for

Probe resets the chip, checks the product ID and runs ad9361_setup, the calibration sequence: BBPLL, synthesiser charge pumps, both RF PLLs, the baseband filters, then baseband DC offset, RF DC offset, TX quadrature, the tracking loops, and finally the ENSM mode and transmit attenuation. Afterwards, on a retune:

RX LO changes
The gain table is reloaded if the band changed. No calibration runs — the driver leaves it to the tracking loops.
TX LO changes
A transmit quadrature calibration runs only if the frequency moved more than 100 MHz (cal_threshold_freq = 100000000ULL). Small retunes reuse the old calibration — part of why image rejection wanders by up to 10 dB between runs.

Two things that will cost you an afternoon

Writing an adi,* file changes nothing — until it changes everything
Those files edit the driver's in-memory copy of the device tree; nothing reaches the chip. Then echo 1 > initialize does a full reset and re-setup: both RF PLLs, every calibration, and the transmit attenuation from the device tree. The vendor's tree says 10 dB — an ungated jump from the −89.75 dB floor to roughly +9 dBm at the connector. This repo's trees say 89750 mdB, maximum attenuation. But on the factory kernel initialize also wipes the cached attenuation to zero, so the next transmit buffer opened restores full output (fixed on the modern target by firmware-modern/patches/0019). Treat initialize as a transmit command, and on the factory kernel re-mute and read both attenuations back after it.
The SPI soft reset does not work, and the driver says so
Verbatim from ad9361_reset: "SPI Soft Reset was removed from the register map, since it doesn't work reliably. Without a prober HW reset randomness may happen. Please specify a RESET GPIO." Without reset-gpios it logs "this may cause unpredicted behavior!" and returns -ENODEV — and probe ignores the return value. You get a half-reset chip and a probe that reports success.

The theory underneath: detection, estimation, information

The matched filter is optimal. Correlation gain is 10·log₁₀(N). MMSE beats zero-forcing. Capacity is B·log₂(1+S/N). Here is why each is true, and where each stops being true.

Detection is a hypothesis test

"Is there a packet here?" has two hypotheses — H₀, only noise; H₁, signal plus noise — and two ways to be wrong:

False alarm
You declare a packet that is not there. Costs you a wasted decode.
Miss
A real packet goes unnoticed. Costs you the packet.

The optimal rule is the likelihood ratio test: form Λ(x) = p₁(x)/p₀(x) and compare it to a threshold. The Neyman–Pearson lemma says that among all tests with a false-alarm rate at most α, it has the highest probability of detection.

Sweep the threshold and you trace the ROC curve — detection probability against false-alarm probability. For a known signal in Gaussian noise it collapses to one number:

the entire ROC, in one parameterdeflection
P_D = Q( Q^-1(P_FA) - d )      d = sqrt( N * A^2 / sigma^2 )

Everything about your data enters through d, which grows as √N: lesson 32's correlation gain, from the statistics side.

Why the matched filter is optimal, in one inequality

Write the filter and the signal as vectors; the output SNR at time T is a ratio of an inner product to a norm. Cauchy–Schwarz — |⟨g,s⟩|² ≤ ⟨g,g⟩·⟨s,s⟩, with equality if and only if g = c·s — gives:

the bound, and when it is metAWGN
SNR(T)  <=  2E / N0            E = the signal's energy

equality iff  h(t) = c * conj( s(T - t) )  // time-reversed and conjugated: the matched filter

So the peak SNR depends only on the signal's energy, not its shape: any waveform of the same energy reaches 2E/N₀. That is why lesson 30 could choose the pulse shape on bandwidth grounds alone.

Two things the matched filter does not do

It is not optimal in coloured noise
The proof assumes white noise. Against a narrowband interferer, or after any filter that shaped the noise, whiten first.
It does not minimise intersymbol interference
Matched filtering is optimal detection; ISI-freedom is the Nyquist criterion on the end-to-end pulse. With ISI the matched filter's samples still keep all the information, but symbol-by-symbol decisions on them are no longer optimal — that needs equalisation (lesson 34) or sequence detection. Root-raised-cosine at each end (lesson 30) satisfies both conditions by design.

Three different "6 dB per bit" rules, and how to keep them apart

  1. Converter resolution — SNR = 6.02·N + 1.76 dB. One more ADC bit buys 6 dB. Lesson 9. Correct.
  2. Capacity per real dimension — C = ½·log₂(1+SNR), so one more bit per dimension needs four times the SNR: 6.02 dB. Correct.
  3. QAM per symbol — a complex symbol is two dimensions, so one more bit per symbol needs only twice the SNR: 3.01 dB (Forney: in the bandwidth-limited regime, 3 dB more SNR buys one more bit per two dimensions).

The table above is the third case: 3.0 to 3.4 dB per bit. Rule 1 or 2 overpredicts a QAM step by a factor of two in decibels.

Estimation: how well can you possibly know a number?

The Cramér–Rao lower bound: an unbiased estimator's variance is at least the reciprocal of the Fisher information, which measures how sharply the likelihood peaks. For the frequency of a complex tone from N samples at SNR ρ, exactly at every N:

frequency estimation CRLBf in cycles per sample
var(f_hat)  >=  6 / ( (2*pi)^2 * rho * N * (N^2 - 1) )

Note the N³: the standard deviation falls as N1.5, so doubling the preamble buys 9 dB of frequency accuracy, not 3 — a longer observation gives the phase more time to rotate, and the rotation carries the frequency. At 61.44 MSPS and 10 dB of SNR:

PreambleBest possible frequency accuracy
64 samples14.8 kHz

4096 samples28.9 Hz

A 64-sample preamble leaves about 15 kHz of uncertainty, only 5.6° of rotation across those 64 samples; it reaches a quarter turn at around 1040 samples. The estimate improves with correlation length, the residual offset (lesson 32's trap) punishes it, and the preamble you want is where the two cross.

The bound is a bound, not a promise — and it has a cliff

The CRLB applies to unbiased estimators only, and may be unattainable at any finite N. Frequency estimation also has a threshold effect: below some SNR the estimate lands in a wrong part of the spectrum, and the bound says nothing about that regime.

Why MMSE beats zero-forcing, in one term

the only differencelinear equalisers, schematically
zero forcing   W  ~  1 / H
MMSE           W  ~  1 / ( H + 1/SNR_mfb )
                              ^^^^^^^^^^^

At a channel null zero-forcing divides by nearly zero, amplifying noise without limit; the extra term (the reciprocal of the matched-filter-bound SNR; exact form in Cioffi's Stanford notes) keeps the denominator away from zero. So the unbiased MMSE equaliser performs at least as well as zero-forcing at every SNR — but its output is biased, which must be removed before slicing or generating LLRs, and it needs an SNR estimate, failing as quietly as lesson 36's sum-product decoder when that estimate is wrong.

Information theory, and the number everybody quotes wrong

Capacity is the mutual information — how much the output tells you about the input — of the best input distribution. Shannon (1948) proved that below capacity a code with arbitrarily few errors exists, by averaging over random codes; it took until turbo codes in 1993 to build one that came close (lesson 36). His AWGN capacity, C = W log((P+N)/N), rewritten in energy per bit and spectral efficiency η = Rb/B, is the condition every link must satisfy:

the real Shannon limitη = bits per second per hertz
Eb/N0  >=  (2^eta - 1) / eta

as eta -> 0:   -> ln 2 = 0.693  ->  10*log10(0.693) = -1.59 dB

−1.59 dB is not your limit, and it is not close

That number is the limit as spectral efficiency goes to zero. At the efficiency you actually run at, it is much higher:

What none of this promises

Capacity says nothing about latency or complexity: approaching it needs long blocks. All of it is about N → ∞, and at a packet radio's short blocks the finite-length penalty is large. All of it assumes white Gaussian noise, which multipath, phase noise and amplifier non-linearity are not. And Neyman–Pearson optimality needs both distributions fully known; with unknown amplitude, phase and timing there is generally no uniformly best test. Finally, Eb/N0 is not SNR: they differ by 10·log₁₀(k), 6 dB for 16-QAM — state the bandwidth with every ratio (lesson 40).

Check yourself: you double your preamble from 512 to 1024 samples. What improves, and by how much?

Detection improves by 3 dB (10·log₁₀(N)); frequency estimation by 9 dB (variance as 1/N³). Past a point a longer coherent correlation stops helping detection, because the residual offset rotates the phase across the window.

The rules worth taping to the wall

Every one of these was learned the expensive way on this exact board.

  • Delete the Vivado project before any HDL or coefficient change, or your change is silently ignored.
  • Simulate before you synthesise. One second against twenty minutes.
  • Gate on the valid strobe, never on l_clk alone — this board is 2R2T, so a sample arrives every second edge.
  • Capture on fifo_rd_valid | fifo_rd_underflow, never on fifo_rd_en, which is the request and arrives a cycle early.
  • Synchronise every control bit that crosses a clock domain, and constrain the crossing with an explicit -from.
  • Verify before you flash, and ./devkit verify --board after — it is the only thing that proves the board runs what you built.
  • Never flash with DFU. Use ./devkit flash over the network, or write the SD card.
  • Set TX attenuation after the buffer opens, then read it back. Writing it before a stream starts guarantees nothing.
  • Never loop transmit back to receive without at least 20 dB of attenuation. The receive port survives about +2.5 dBm; the transmitter reaches about +19 dBm.
  • Look at the port, then affirm it: ./devkit tx-guard affirm 0 (TX1A) or 1 (TX2A) before anything raises output. A reboot withdraws it.
  • Do not trust a cyclic transmit to stop by itself. It outlives the program that started it; the modern (Debian) target bounds it at 60 s, the factory target not at all.
  • A guarantee you cannot reproduce on the bench is not a guarantee. Killing a transmitting program was supposed to mute the radio; measured, the port sat 12.6 dB hotter than muted with the program gone. The fix keys off a state, not an event: no data reaching the converter for 250 ms means mute.
  • Zero is not a safe default. A reset path that memsets the cached transmit attenuation turns it into 0 dB — full output — on the next stream. Fixed on the modern target; still live on the factory kernel after a debugfs initialize.
  • When the radio misbehaves, check /mnt/jffs2 first. It is writable, it survives reflashing, and a script there can rewrite settings underneath your application.
